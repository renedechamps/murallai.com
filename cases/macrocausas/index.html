Macrocausas: Navigating Multi-Defendant Criminal Proceedings in Spain

Last updated: May 2026 · By the Muralla AI legal research team
TL;DR — A macrocausa (macro-proceeding) is a Spanish criminal case of exceptional scale — often involving fifty or more defendants, tens of thousands of folios (pages) of evidence, and investigation periods spanning years. These proceedings arise from corruption investigations, banking scandals, and organised crime prosecutions. For international firms with a client caught in a macrocausa, the challenges are procedural, logistical, and strategic: managing an overwhelming case file, coordinating with dozens of co-defendant teams, and making critical decisions about conformidad (plea agreements) — all in Spanish. This guide explains how macrocausas work and how to defend effectively within them.

What Makes a Case a Macrocausa?

There is no formal statutory definition of a macrocausa in Spanish procedural law. The term is used by practitioners, courts, and legal commentators to describe criminal proceedings that exceed ordinary scales of complexity. While the boundaries are imprecise, a proceeding is generally considered a macrocausa when it exhibits several of the following characteristics.

First, the number of defendants. Macrocausas typically involve fifty or more investigados (investigated persons, the term that replaced imputados after the 2015 procedural reform). Cases exceeding one hundred defendants are not uncommon, and the largest have involved several hundred.

Second, the volume of documentary evidence. The case file — the sumario (judicial investigation file) or diligencias previas (preliminary proceedings) — may comprise tens of thousands of folios. In major cases, the file can exceed one hundred thousand folios, encompassing financial records, corporate documents, intercepted communications, expert reports, and witness statements.

Third, the duration of the investigation. The fase de instrucción (investigation phase) in a macrocausa routinely extends over three to six years. The entire proceeding — from the initial investigation to final appeal — can span a decade or more. Delays are endemic and affect all parties.

Fourth, the multiplicity of charges. Macrocausas typically involve numerous interconnected offences: corruption (cohecho), embezzlement (malversación), fraud (estafa), money laundering (blanqueo de capitales), and tax fraud (delito fiscal) frequently appear together. The charges may be organised in separate piezas separadas (separate sections) within the overall proceeding, each addressing a distinct cluster of facts and defendants.

What Types of Cases Become Macrocausas?

Three categories of cases account for the majority of Spanish macrocausas.

Corruption Proceedings

Spain's most prominent macrocausas have arisen from investigations into political and institutional corruption. These cases typically involve allegations of cohecho (bribery), prevaricación (abuse of public authority), tráfico de influencias (influence peddling), and associated money laundering. They often begin with a single line of investigation — a suspicious public contract, an unexplained payment — and expand over years as investigators follow financial trails that implicate additional actors. The involvement of public officials triggers jurisdiction in the Audiencia Nacional (National Court) or, for senior officeholders, the Tribunal Supremo (Supreme Court).

Banking and Financial Crime

The restructuring of Spain's banking sector following the financial crisis generated several macrocausas. These proceedings involve allegations of administración desleal (unfaithful administration), falsedad contable (accounting fraud), and estafa affecting thousands of shareholders, depositors, or holders of preferential instruments. The cases are distinguished by the complexity of the financial products at issue and the number of affected parties, some of whom constitute themselves as acusación particular (private prosecution) or acción popular (public interest prosecution).

Organised Crime

Large-scale drug trafficking and organised crime investigations frequently produce macrocausas. These cases involve extensive wiretap evidence, international cooperation, and multiple defendants operating across jurisdictions. The Audiencia Nacional has primary jurisdiction over organised crime, and its courts are experienced in managing proceedings of this scale.

What Procedural Challenges Define a Macrocausa?

Managing the Case File

The fundamental challenge in any macrocausa is the sheer volume of the case file. International firms accustomed to electronic discovery and structured databases will find that Spanish macrocausas often involve a combination of digitised and physical documents, with indexing that ranges from systematic to chaotic depending on the court. The Ley de Enjuiciamiento Criminal (Criminal Procedure Act) grants defence teams the right to access the full case file once the secreto de sumario (judicial secrecy) is lifted, but exercising that right effectively requires a system for processing, indexing, and searching the material.

Each pieza separada (separate section) may run to thousands of folios, and the relevance of material in one section to a defendant charged in another is not always immediately apparent. Cross-referencing between sections is essential but labour-intensive. Failure to identify exculpatory material buried in a distant section of the file is a real and recurring risk.

Coordinating Among Multiple Defence Teams

A macrocausa with fifty defendants may involve thirty or more separate defence teams, each representing one or more clients. The relationships between defendants vary: some are aligned, others are antagonistic, and many shift over the course of the proceedings. For an international firm representing a single defendant, understanding the broader landscape — who is cooperating with prosecutors, who is pursuing a conformidad, who is likely to testify against co-defendants — is critical to strategic decision-making.

Spanish procedural rules do not provide a formal mechanism for inter-defence coordination, but informal communication among defence counsel is standard practice. International firms must navigate this landscape carefully, balancing the potential benefits of coordination against the risks of conflicts of interest and inadvertent disclosure of privileged information.

The Role of the Acusación Particular and Acción Popular

Unlike common-law systems, Spanish criminal proceedings permit victims to participate actively as acusación particular (private prosecution) and allow any citizen to exercise acción popular (public interest prosecution). In macrocausas, this can mean dozens of additional parties with independent procedural rights — the right to propose evidence, examine witnesses, and make sentencing submissions. Managing the procedural dynamics created by multiple prosecution actors is a distinct challenge that has no direct analogue in adversarial systems.

How Should Defence Teams Approach Strategy in a Macrocausa?

Early Assessment and Triage

The first priority upon entering a macrocausa is a thorough assessment of the client's position within the broader case. This requires identifying every reference to the client in the case file — across all piezas separadas — and mapping the factual allegations, the evidence supporting them, and the specific charges. In a case file spanning tens of thousands of folios, this triage process traditionally takes weeks or months of manual review by Spanish-qualified lawyers.

Isolation Strategy vs. Joint Defence

A critical strategic choice is whether to pursue an isolation strategy — distancing the client from co-defendants and contesting the client's alleged role — or to participate in a coordinated joint defence. The choice depends on the client's specific position, the strength of the evidence against them, and the strategies of aligned and adverse co-defendants. In many macrocausas, the optimal approach evolves over time as new evidence emerges and co-defendants adjust their positions.

The Conformidad Question

The question of whether to pursue conformidad (a plea agreement) is often the most consequential strategic decision in a macrocausa. Under Spanish procedural law, a defendant can reach an individual conformidad with the Ministerio Fiscal (public prosecution) without requiring agreement from co-defendants. For sentences of up to six years, conformidad can secure a one-third reduction in the sentence.

The timing of a conformidad is strategically critical. Early agreements — reached during the instrucción — may secure the most favourable terms, as prosecutors value early resolution. However, early agreement also means accepting a sentence before the full case against the client has crystallised, creating a risk of agreeing to terms that a contested trial might have improved upon. For international clients, the decision must account for the cross-border consequences of a guilty plea, including potential recognition of the Spanish conviction in the client's home jurisdiction.

Managing the Juicio Oral

If the case proceeds to juicio oral (oral trial), the logistics are formidable. The trial in a major macrocausa may extend over months of court sessions, with dozens of defendants, hundreds of witnesses, and thousands of documentary exhibits. The trial takes place before a panel of the Audiencia Nacional or the relevant Audiencia Provincial (Provincial Court), and the proceedings follow the inquisitorial model — the presiding judge actively questions witnesses, and the order of examination differs substantially from common-law practice.

For international firms coordinating with Spanish trial counsel, the key is to ensure that the defence strategy developed during the instrucción translates effectively into the oral trial format. This requires advance preparation of examination outlines, identification of critical documentary exhibits, and coordination with expert witnesses — all of which must account for the specific procedural rules governing the juicio oral.

What Are the Common Outcomes in Macrocausas?

Outcomes in macrocausas are highly variable. Some defendants are acquitted, others receive suspended sentences, and still others receive substantial prison terms. The outcomes for co-defendants within the same macrocausa can vary enormously depending on their individual roles, the evidence against them, and their strategic choices.

Acquittal rates in macrocausas are not negligible. The complexity of these proceedings, the length of the investigation, and the volume of evidence create genuine opportunities for defence challenges — evidentiary exclusions, statute of limitations arguments, and challenges to the subjective element of the charged offences. Spanish courts have acquitted defendants in major macrocausas on the basis of insufficiency of evidence, even where co-defendants were convicted on the same factual matrix.

Appeals in macrocausas are virtually automatic. Convictions from the Audiencia Nacional are appealed to its Sala de Apelación (Appeals Chamber) and then potentially to the Tribunal Supremo. The appellate process in a macrocausa can add two to four years to the overall timeline, during which the sentence is not executed (absent preventive detention). For international clients, this extended timeline creates both opportunities and uncertainty.
