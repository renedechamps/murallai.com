Money Laundering Charges in Spain: Defence Strategies for International Clients

Last updated: May 2026 · By the Muralla AI legal research team
TL;DR — Spain prosecutes money laundering under Articles 301–304 of the Código Penal, with sentences of up to six years for basic offences and nine for aggravated cases. A conviction for the predicate offence is not required. Investigations typically originate with Sepblac (Spain's Financial Intelligence Unit) and proceed through an instrucción (investigation) led by a judge. International clients face particular risks from cross-border information-sharing and expansive Spanish judicial cooperation. This guide covers what firms need to know to mount an effective defence.

What Constitutes Money Laundering Under Spanish Law?

Blanqueo de capitales (money laundering) is defined under Article 301 of Spain's Código Penal (Criminal Code). The offence captures anyone who acquires, possesses, uses, converts, or transmits assets knowing that they originate from a criminal offence, or who performs any other act designed to conceal or disguise the illicit origin of those assets. The provision is deliberately broad, and Spanish courts have interpreted it expansively.

Article 301 also extends to those who conceal or disguise the true nature, source, location, disposition, movement, or rights relating to assets, knowing they derive from criminal activity. Crucially, this includes so-called "self-laundering" — where the person who committed the predicate offence also launders the proceeds. Spain is among the European jurisdictions that criminalise self-laundering explicitly, a point of particular importance for international clients who may face parallel proceedings in jurisdictions that do not.

Articles 302 through 304 address aggravated forms and ancillary provisions. Article 302 increases penalties when laundering is committed by members of criminal organisations or when the offence involves particularly large sums. Article 303 addresses negligent money laundering — a provision that catches professionals (bankers, lawyers, notaries) who fail to exercise due diligence. Article 304 allows courts to impose penalties even when the predicate offence was committed outside Spain, provided it constitutes an offence under Spanish law.

What Scenarios Typically Trigger Investigations Against International Clients?

The cases involving international defendants tend to follow recognisable patterns. Understanding these patterns is the first step toward effective defence preparation.

International Wire Transfers and Banking Irregularities

The most common trigger involves suspicious patterns in cross-border wire transfers. Spanish banks are required under Ley 10/2010 de prevención del blanqueo de capitales (Law 10/2010 on the prevention of money laundering) to file comunicaciones por indicio (suspicious transaction reports) with Sepblac when transactions lack clear economic justification. For international clients, round-tripping of funds between jurisdictions, rapid movement of large sums through Spanish accounts, or deposits inconsistent with declared income frequently trigger these reports.

Real Estate Acquisitions

Spain's property market has long attracted scrutiny from money laundering investigators. Purchases of luxury real estate — particularly on the Mediterranean coast and in major cities — by non-resident buyers through corporate vehicles attract heightened attention. Investigators look for purchases significantly below or above market value, rapid resale, use of offshore companies to obscure beneficial ownership, and cash payments or payments from multiple unrelated sources.

Corporate Structures and Shell Companies

The use of layered corporate structures involving Spanish sociedades limitadas (limited liability companies) or sociedades anónimas (public limited companies) in combination with offshore entities raises immediate red flags. Investigators are experienced at tracing funds through multi-jurisdictional corporate chains, and the Spanish Registro Mercantil (Commercial Registry) provides a starting point for unravelling these structures. The 2022 reforms to Spain's beneficial ownership registry have further strengthened investigators' tools.

How Does the Investigation Process Work?

Understanding the procedural framework is essential for international firms representing clients in Spanish blanqueo cases. The investigation follows a distinct sequence that differs materially from common-law systems.

The Role of Sepblac

Sepblac — the Servicio Ejecutivo de la Comisión de Prevención del Blanqueo de Capitales e Infracciones Monetarias (Executive Service of the Commission for the Prevention of Money Laundering and Monetary Offences) — serves as Spain's Financial Intelligence Unit. Financial institutions, notaries, auditors, and other sujetos obligados (obligated subjects) must report suspicious transactions to Sepblac. The unit analyses these reports, enriches them with data from other sources, and refers actionable intelligence to the Fiscalía (public prosecution service) or directly to investigating courts.

The Juez de Instrucción and the Investigation Phase

Once a criminal investigation is opened, it falls under the control of a juez de instrucción (investigating judge). This is a fundamental distinction from adversarial systems. The investigating judge is not a passive arbiter — they actively direct the investigation, order searches and seizures, authorise wiretaps, issue international rogatorias (letters rogatory), and take witness statements. The judge's role is theoretically neutral, but in practice the defence must actively engage during this fase de instrucción (investigation phase) to protect the client's interests.

International cases routinely involve comisiones rogatorias (letters rogatory) to obtain evidence from foreign jurisdictions. Under EU instruments — notably the European Investigation Order — this process has become significantly faster than in the past. Swiss, Liechtenstein, and Panamanian banking records can take longer, but Spanish judges are persistent and experienced in obtaining them.

Provisional Measures and Asset Freezing

Spanish courts have extensive powers to freeze assets during the investigation phase. Under the Ley de Enjuiciamiento Criminal (Criminal Procedure Act), the investigating judge can order the embargo preventivo (preventive attachment) of bank accounts, real estate, vehicles, and other assets suspected of being proceeds of crime. These measures can be imposed without prior notice to the defendant, and they can remain in place for the duration of the investigation — which in complex money laundering cases can extend over several years.

What Are the Most Effective Defence Strategies?

Defending against blanqueo de capitales charges requires a strategy tailored to the specific facts of the case, but several lines of defence recur across matters.

Demonstrating Legitimate Origin of Funds

The single most powerful defence is demonstrating that the funds have a legitimate origin. This requires assembling comprehensive documentary evidence — tax returns, business records, loan agreements, inheritance documentation, investment histories — that traces the funds back to a lawful source. For international clients, this often means coordinating the collection and authentication of documents from multiple jurisdictions. Spanish courts will accept foreign documents, but they must typically be apostilled or legalised and, in many cases, accompanied by sworn translations (traducciones juradas).

Challenging Knowledge and Intent

Article 301 requires that the defendant knew or should have known that the assets derived from criminal activity. This subjective element — dolo (intent) — offers a significant line of defence, particularly in cases involving legitimate business people who may have been unwitting participants in laundering schemes. The defence must demonstrate that the client had no actual knowledge of the illicit origin of the funds and that no reasonable person in their position would have suspected it. This is particularly relevant in cases involving complex financial products, intermediated transactions, or reliance on professional advisors.

Chain of Custody and Evidentiary Challenges

Spanish criminal procedure requires that evidence be obtained in accordance with fundamental rights. Evidence obtained in violation of these rights is subject to exclusion under the doctrine of prueba ilícita (unlawfully obtained evidence). Defence counsel should scrutinise the chain of custody for all documentary evidence, the legality of any intercepted communications, and the procedural correctness of international evidence-gathering. Defects in rogatorias — particularly failures to comply with the procedural requirements of the requested state — can provide grounds for exclusion.

Challenging the Predicate Offence

While Spanish law does not require a prior conviction for the predicate offence, the prosecution must prove that the assets originated from criminal activity. In cases where the alleged predicate offence occurred in another jurisdiction, the defence can challenge whether the conduct actually constitutes a criminal offence under Spanish law (the requirement of doble incriminación — dual criminality). This is a technical but potentially decisive argument, particularly where the alleged predicate offence involves regulatory violations that may not have direct equivalents in Spanish criminal law.

What Sentences Do Spanish Courts Impose for Money Laundering?

The sentencing framework for blanqueo de capitales is structured around the basic and aggravated forms of the offence. For the basic offence under Article 301, courts may impose imprisonment of six months to six years and a fine proportional to the value of the laundered assets (up to three times their value). The aggravated form under Article 302 — applicable where the offender belongs to a criminal organisation or the laundering involves large-scale operations — increases the upper limit to nine years.

Beyond imprisonment and fines, courts can impose inhabilitación (disqualification) from professional activities for two to five years. For corporate defendants, consequences include fines, dissolution, suspension of activities, judicial intervention, or prohibition from obtaining public subsidies. Asset confiscation (decomiso) is mandatory upon conviction and extends to all assets of illicit origin, including substitute assets where the original proceeds have been dissipated.

In practice, sentences for international clients tend to cluster in the three-to-five-year range for basic offences, with aggravated cases occasionally exceeding six years. Courts consider mitigating factors including cooperation with authorities, absence of prior criminal record, and voluntary restitution. The possibility of suspensión de la pena (suspended sentence) exists for sentences of two years or less, which can be a realistic objective in cases involving peripheral involvement.

What Recent Trends Should International Firms Monitor?

Several developments in Spanish anti-money laundering enforcement merit close attention from international practitioners.

First, Spain has significantly expanded its use of European Investigation Orders and other international cooperation mechanisms. The time required to obtain foreign banking records has decreased substantially, and Spanish judges now routinely coordinate with counterparts across the EU through Eurojust and the European Judicial Network.

Second, the Fiscalía Especial contra la Corrupción y la Criminalidad Organizada (Special Prosecutor's Office against Corruption and Organised Crime) has increased its focus on professional enablers — lawyers, accountants, and corporate service providers who facilitate laundering schemes. This trend means that advisory professionals themselves face increased exposure.

Third, Spanish courts have shown willingness to apply extended confiscation powers, allowing the seizure of assets beyond those directly linked to the charged offence where there is a disproportionate gap between the defendant's legitimate income and their total asset holdings. This expanded decomiso ampliado (extended confiscation) significantly raises the stakes for defendants.

Fourth, cryptocurrency and digital asset laundering has emerged as a major enforcement priority. Spanish authorities have developed specialised units to trace blockchain transactions, and recent legislative changes have brought virtual asset service providers within the scope of anti-money laundering obligations.
