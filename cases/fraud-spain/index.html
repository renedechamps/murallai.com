Fraud & Economic Crime in Spain: A Guide for International Firms

Last updated: May 2026 · By the Muralla AI legal research team
TL;DR — Spain's Código Penal addresses economic crime across multiple provisions: estafa (fraud, Art. 248–251), delito fiscal (tax fraud, Art. 305), falsedad documental (document forgery, Art. 390–399), alzamiento de bienes (asset concealment, Art. 257–258), and administración desleal (unfaithful administration, Art. 252). Sentences range from one to eight years depending on the offence and aggravating factors. International firms must understand how these charges interact, as prosecutors frequently layer multiple offences in a single case. This guide covers each offence category, defence strategies, and current enforcement trends.

What Is Estafa and How Is It Prosecuted in Spain?

Estafa (fraud) is defined under Article 248 of the Código Penal as the use of deception (engaño bastante — sufficient deception) to cause another person to make a disposition of assets to their own detriment or that of a third party, with the intent to obtain an unlawful enrichment. The deception must be the proximate cause of the victim's act of disposition — a causal chain that the prosecution must establish beyond reasonable doubt.

Article 249 provides the basic sentencing framework: imprisonment of six months to three years for the basic form. Article 250 defines aggravated estafa, which applies when the fraud involves amounts exceeding fifty thousand euros, affects a large number of victims, exploits the personal circumstances of the victim, or is committed through the abuse of business relationships. Aggravated estafa carries sentences of one to six years and fines of six to twelve months.

For international clients, estafa charges frequently arise in the context of investment schemes marketed to Spanish investors, contractual fraud in international commercial transactions, and insurance fraud involving Spanish insurers. The key element that distinguishes estafa from a mere civil breach of contract is the presence of deception from the outset — the engaño must precede or accompany the contractual relationship, not emerge afterwards.

How Does Spain Prosecute Tax Fraud?

Delito fiscal (tax fraud) under Article 305 is one of the most actively prosecuted economic crimes in Spain and carries particular significance for international clients with Spanish tax obligations. The offence requires a defrauded amount exceeding one hundred and twenty thousand euros per tax year and per taxable event. Below this threshold, the conduct constitutes an administrative infraction rather than a criminal offence.

The basic offence carries imprisonment of one to five years plus a fine of up to six times the defrauded amount. An aggravated form — applicable when the defrauded amount exceeds six hundred thousand euros, when the fraud is committed through criminal organisations, or when personas interpuestas (interposed persons) or paraísos fiscales (tax havens) are used to conceal the fraud — increases the sentence range to two to six years.

A critical feature of Spanish tax fraud law is the regularización voluntaria (voluntary regularisation) exemption. Article 305.4 provides that a taxpayer who voluntarily regularises their tax position before being notified of the commencement of an investigation is exempt from criminal liability. This provision creates a narrow but important window for international clients who discover potential Spanish tax exposure. The timing is critical: once the Agencia Tributaria (Tax Agency) or the Fiscalía initiates proceedings, the exemption is no longer available.

What Constitutes Document Forgery Under Spanish Law?

Falsedad documental (document forgery) is addressed in Articles 390 through 399 of the Código Penal. The provisions distinguish between forgery of public documents (documentos públicos), official documents (documentos oficiales), and commercial or private documents (documentos mercantiles o privados), with escalating penalties reflecting the gravity of each category.

Under Article 392, a private individual who forges a public, official, or commercial document faces imprisonment of six months to three years plus a fine. The forgery must involve one of the acts listed in Article 390.1: altering a genuine document, simulating a document in whole or in part, assuming another's identity, or making an untruthful statement in a document. In economic crime cases, falsedad documental frequently accompanies estafa charges — forged invoices, false financial statements, fabricated contracts, and manipulated accounting records are common exhibits.

For international firms, the interplay between falsedad and other economic offences is a critical consideration. Spanish prosecutors routinely charge falsedad as a delito medio (instrumental offence) — a crime committed as a means to accomplish another crime — which can affect the calculation of the overall sentence through the rules on concurso medial (instrumental combination of offences).

How Are Asset Concealment and Unfaithful Administration Charged?

Alzamiento de Bienes (Asset Concealment)

Alzamiento de bienes (asset concealment or fraudulent conveyance) under Articles 257–258 of the Código Penal criminalises the act of concealing, disposing of, or diminishing one's assets with the intent to frustrate the claims of creditors. The offence carries imprisonment of one to four years and a fine of twelve to twenty-four months.

This offence is particularly relevant in international corporate insolvency scenarios. When a Spanish company facing financial difficulty transfers assets abroad, restructures ownership through foreign entities, or liquidates assets at below-market values to related parties, investigators will examine whether these transactions constitute alzamiento. The prosecution must prove that the defendant's purpose was to defraud creditors — mere insolvency or poor financial management is insufficient.

Administración Desleal (Unfaithful Administration)

Administración desleal (unfaithful administration) under Article 252, as reformed in 2015, captures anyone who, having been entrusted with the administration of another's assets or patrimony, exceeds their authority or breaches their duties of loyal management, thereby causing financial harm to the principal. The offence carries the same penalties as estafa: six months to three years for the basic form, one to six years in aggravated cases.

The 2015 reform separated administración desleal from the former apropiación indebida (misappropriation) provision, creating a distinct offence focused on breaches of fiduciary duty. For international directors serving on the boards of Spanish companies or subsidiaries, this offence is a primary source of personal criminal exposure. Actions that might constitute a mere breach of fiduciary duty in common-law jurisdictions can trigger criminal prosecution in Spain.

How Are Economic Crime Investigations Conducted in Spain?

Economic crime investigations in Spain follow the inquisitorial model, directed by the juez de instrucción (investigating judge). The investigation typically begins with a denuncia (complaint) — filed by a victim, a regulatory body such as the Agencia Tributaria, or by the Fiscalía itself following preliminary inquiries. In tax fraud cases, the Agencia Tributaria's inspectors often conduct extensive administrative investigations before referring matters for criminal prosecution.

During the fase de instrucción (investigation phase), the investigating judge has broad powers: ordering searches of premises and electronic devices, freezing bank accounts through embargo preventivo (preventive attachment), authorising the interception of communications, and compelling testimony from witnesses and suspects. International elements — offshore accounts, foreign corporate records, communications with overseas counterparts — are obtained through comisiones rogatorias (letters rogatory) or, within the EU, through European Investigation Orders.

A distinctive feature of Spanish economic crime proceedings is the role of the acusación particular (private prosecution). Victims of fraud and economic crime have the right to constitute themselves as private prosecutors, gaining full access to the case file and the ability to propose charges and evidence independently of the Ministerio Fiscal (public prosecution). In major fraud cases, this can result in multiple prosecution actors with different interests and strategies, complicating the procedural landscape for the defence.

What Defence Strategies Apply Across Economic Crime Cases?

Challenging the Subjective Element

All Spanish economic offences require proof of dolo (criminal intent). In complex commercial settings, the line between aggressive business conduct and criminal fraud is often contested. The defence can argue that the client acted in good faith, relied on professional advice, or lacked the specific intent required by the charged offence. Documentary evidence of the client's genuine business purposes, reliance on legal and financial advisors, and compliance with standard industry practices can be decisive.

Disputing the Quantification of Harm

In estafa and delito fiscal cases, the quantification of economic harm directly affects both the applicability of aggravating factors and the sentence. Defence teams should rigorously challenge prosecution calculations, engaging independent forensic accountants to contest valuation methodologies, identify offsetting benefits, and question assumptions underlying damage estimates. In tax fraud cases specifically, the distinction between legitimate tax planning and criminal evasion often hinges on technical interpretations of Spanish tax law — areas where the defence can engage specialist tax counsel to provide expert testimony challenging the prosecution's characterisation of the client's arrangements.

Statute of Limitations

The prescripción (statute of limitations) is a frequently viable defence in economic crime cases, particularly given the lengthy investigation periods common in complex financial matters. The limitation period is determined by the maximum penalty for the offence: five years for offences carrying up to five years imprisonment, ten years for more serious offences. The critical question is when the limitation period begins to run and what actions interrupt it — areas where Spanish jurisprudence has developed nuanced rules.

Conformidad (Plea Agreement)

The conformidad (plea agreement) mechanism under the Ley de Enjuiciamiento Criminal (Criminal Procedure Act) allows defendants to accept a reduced sentence in exchange for an admission of facts. For sentences of up to six years, conformidad can secure a one-third reduction. In multi-defendant economic crime cases, early engagement with this mechanism — ideally before the juicio oral (oral trial) — can achieve significantly more favourable outcomes than contested proceedings.

What Are the Current Enforcement Trends in Spanish Economic Crime?

Several trends define the current landscape. First, the Fiscalía has significantly increased coordination with the Agencia Tributaria and international tax authorities under the Common Reporting Standard, leading to a surge in delito fiscal prosecutions targeting international taxpayers with undeclared Spanish assets.

Second, prosecutors are increasingly using administración desleal charges against directors of international companies operating in Spain, particularly in the wake of corporate failures. The 2015 reform broadened the offence, and courts are still defining its boundaries through case law.

Third, digital evidence has transformed economic crime investigations. Spanish courts now routinely admit electronic records, email correspondence, encrypted communications, and digital forensic evidence. The Policía Nacional and Guardia Civil have developed specialised units with sophisticated digital forensic capabilities.

Fourth, the interaction between criminal proceedings and procedimientos concursales (insolvency proceedings) has become increasingly complex. The Spanish courts regularly address situations where criminal alzamiento de bienes charges run parallel to civil insolvency actions, creating jurisdictional and strategic challenges for defence teams managing both proceedings simultaneously.

Fifth, cross-border cooperation in tax fraud cases has reached unprecedented levels. Spain's implementation of the EU's DAC6 directive — requiring intermediaries to report certain cross-border tax arrangements — has created a new stream of intelligence feeding into criminal investigations. International structures involving Spanish entities are subject to heightened scrutiny, and the automatic exchange of financial account information under the Common Reporting Standard means that undeclared Spanish assets held abroad are increasingly discoverable.
