Corporate Criminal Liability in Spain: What Directors Need to Know

Last updated: May 2026 · By the Muralla AI legal research team
TL;DR — Spain introduced responsabilidad penal de la persona jurídica (corporate criminal liability) in 2010 through Article 31 bis of the Código Penal, with a major reform in 2015 that established compliance programmes as a potential full defence. Corporations can face fines, dissolution, suspension, and disqualification from public contracts. Individual directors face parallel liability under Article 31 — and both the corporation and the director can be prosecuted simultaneously. For multinational directors with Spanish operations, understanding this dual-liability framework is essential. This guide covers the statutory framework, the compliance defence, individual director exposure, and the cross-border implications that international firms must navigate.

How Did Corporate Criminal Liability Develop in Spain?

Spain's adoption of corporate criminal liability represented a fundamental departure from the traditional civil-law principle of societas delinquere non potest — the doctrine that legal persons cannot commit crimes. The Ley Orgánica 5/2010 (Organic Law 5/2010) introduced Article 31 bis into the Código Penal, creating for the first time a framework under which corporations could be held directly liable for criminal offences.

The initial 2010 regime was widely criticised as incomplete. It established corporate liability but offered no clear mechanism for exculpation. The critical reform came with Ley Orgánica 1/2015 (Organic Law 1/2015), which overhauled Article 31 bis and, for the first time, recognised effective compliance programmes (programas de cumplimiento or modelos de prevención de delitos) as a basis for full exemption from corporate criminal liability. This reform was influenced by international standards, particularly the OECD Good Practice Guidance and comparable regimes in Italy, the United Kingdom, and the United States.

The current framework, in effect since July 2015, establishes two pathways to corporate liability. The first captures offences committed by senior managers — those with authority to make decisions on behalf of the corporation or who hold organisational control (representantes legales or persons acting with autoridad de dirección). The second captures offences committed by employees subject to the authority of those senior managers, where the offence was facilitated by a failure of supervision or control.

Which Offences Can Trigger Corporate Criminal Liability?

Corporate criminal liability in Spain is not universal — it applies only to a closed list of offences specified in the Código Penal. However, this list is extensive and covers the offences most relevant to international business operations. The catalogue includes:

  • Economic offences: estafa (fraud), administración desleal (unfaithful administration), insolvencias punibles (criminal insolvency), delitos contra la Hacienda Pública (tax offences), and blanqueo de capitales (money laundering).
  • Corruption offences: cohecho (bribery of public officials), tráfico de influencias (influence peddling), and corruption in international commercial transactions.
  • Environmental offences: offences against natural resources and the environment under Articles 325–331.
  • Data protection and privacy offences: unlawful access to personal data and violation of privacy rights.
  • Labour exploitation: offences against workers' rights, including trafficking for the purpose of labour exploitation.
  • Terrorist financing and smuggling (contrabando).

For multinational corporations operating in Spain, the breadth of this list means that corporate criminal liability is a consideration across virtually all business activities. The inclusion of bribery, tax fraud, and money laundering makes it directly relevant to standard international compliance programmes.

What Role Do Compliance Programmes Play as a Defence?

The 2015 reform to Article 31 bis introduced the compliance defence — arguably the most important development in Spanish corporate criminal law. Under Article 31 bis(2), a corporation can be fully exempt from criminal liability if it demonstrates that, before the offence was committed, it had adopted and effectively implemented a compliance programme that meets the requirements set out in the statute.

Article 31 bis(5) specifies the elements that an effective programa de cumplimiento (compliance programme) must contain:

  • Risk assessment: Identification of activities within which offences could be committed (mapa de riesgos — risk map).
  • Internal protocols and procedures: Establishment of protocols for decision-making, financial management, and reporting that reduce the risk of offences.
  • Financial resource allocation: Adequate financial resources dedicated to the operation of the compliance programme.
  • Reporting channel: A canal de denuncias (whistleblowing channel) enabling internal reporting of potential non-compliance, with protections for reporters.
  • Disciplinary system: A system of sanctions for breaches of the compliance programme.
  • Periodic verification and review: Regular assessment of the programme's effectiveness and updates to reflect changes in the organisation or the legal landscape.

Critically, the Tribunal Supremo has emphasised in several landmark decisions that the compliance programme must be genuine and effective — a mere paper exercise will not suffice. Courts will examine whether the programme was actually implemented, whether it was proportionate to the organisation's risk profile, and whether the compliance function had genuine independence and authority. The 2016 Circular 1/2016 of the Fiscalía General del Estado (Attorney General's Office) provides detailed guidance on how prosecutors should assess compliance programmes, establishing a framework that defence teams must engage with directly.

For the exemption to apply where the offence was committed by a senior manager, Article 31 bis(2) additionally requires that a supervisory body — the órgano de cumplimiento (compliance officer or compliance committee) — was in place and was exercising its functions at the time of the offence. In smaller companies, this function may be performed by the board of directors itself, but in larger organisations, an independent compliance function is effectively mandatory.

When Do Individual Directors Face Criminal Charges?

Article 31 of the Código Penal provides that a natural person who acts as a administrador de hecho o de derecho (de facto or de jure director) of a legal entity, or who acts on its behalf, can be held individually liable for criminal offences committed in that capacity — even where the qualifying conditions of the offence (such as being a debtor or a taxpayer) are met only by the legal entity itself. This provision is the primary source of individual criminal exposure for directors.

The concept of administrador de hecho (de facto director) is particularly significant for multinational groups. Spanish courts have applied it to foreign nationals who exercise effective control over Spanish subsidiaries, even where they do not hold a formal directorship. A parent company executive who directs the operations of a Spanish subsidiary, approves its major decisions, or controls its financial resources may be treated as a de facto director and thus subject to personal criminal liability under Spanish law.

Article 31 ter of the Código Penal establishes the principle of independent liability: the criminal liability of the corporation is independent of whether the specific individual who committed the offence has been identified or successfully prosecuted. This means that a corporation can be convicted even if the individual perpetrator is unknown. Conversely, it also means that corporate liability does not require individual liability and vice versa — both can coexist independently.

How Does Dual Liability Work in Practice?

The dual-liability framework means that in any investigation involving a Spanish corporation, both the entity and its directors are potentially exposed. Prosecutors routinely charge both, and the strategic dynamics between the corporate and individual defences are complex.

A corporation may seek to argue that the offence was committed by a rogue individual acting outside their authority — thereby limiting the corporate liability. Simultaneously, the individual may argue that they were acting within the corporate framework and that responsibility lies with the organisation's systems and culture. These competing narratives must be managed carefully, particularly when the same law firm represents both the corporation and individual directors — a situation that frequently creates conflicts of interest requiring careful ethical navigation.

For multinational groups, the dual-liability framework creates scenarios in which a Spanish subsidiary, its local directors, and its foreign parent company executives may all face simultaneous criminal proceedings. The investigation may extend to the parent company's records through international cooperation mechanisms, and the question of whether the parent company's compliance programme adequately covered the Spanish subsidiary's operations becomes directly relevant to the defence.

What Penalties Do Corporations Face?

Article 33.7 of the Código Penal establishes a comprehensive catalogue of penalties applicable to convicted corporations. The most frequently imposed penalties include:

  • Fines: Calculated through a multa por cuotas (daily quota fine) system, with the quota amount and duration determined by the gravity of the offence. For the most serious offences, fines can reach several million euros.
  • Suspension of activities: Suspension of all or specific corporate activities for up to five years.
  • Closure of business premises: Temporary closure of specific premises for up to five years.
  • Disqualification from public contracts: Prohibition from obtaining public subsidies, grants, or contracts for up to fifteen years — a penalty that can be more commercially devastating than fines.
  • Judicial intervention: Court-appointed supervision of the corporation's activities for a defined period.
  • Dissolution: Reserved for the most extreme cases — entities created specifically for criminal purposes or whose principal activity constitutes a criminal offence.

In practice, fines and disqualification from public contracts are the most commonly imposed penalties. Dissolution remains rare and is effectively limited to shell companies or entities with no legitimate business purpose. Judicial intervention has been used in cases involving ongoing compliance failures and serves as a mechanism for imposed reform of corporate governance.

What Are the Cross-Border Implications for Multinational Directors?

The cross-border dimensions of Spanish corporate criminal liability are among the most complex aspects of the regime. Several issues merit particular attention from international firms.

First, the question of territorial jurisdiction. Spanish courts can assert jurisdiction over offences committed on Spanish territory, including offences committed through Spanish-registered entities. Where a foreign executive directs conduct from abroad that causes criminal results in Spain, jurisdictional questions become contested — but Spanish courts have shown willingness to assert broad jurisdiction in economic crime cases.

Second, the recognition of foreign compliance programmes. When a multinational corporation's compliance programme was designed and implemented at the group level, the question arises whether it satisfies the requirements of Article 31 bis(5) as applied to the Spanish subsidiary. Spanish courts will assess whether the group-level programme was effectively adapted to Spanish legal requirements, implemented at the subsidiary level, and subject to local oversight. A generic global compliance programme that does not address Spanish-specific risks may be found insufficient.

Third, the implications of a Spanish corporate conviction for the entity's operations in other jurisdictions. A Spanish criminal conviction may trigger debarment from public procurement in EU member states, disclosure obligations under securities regulations in the entity's home jurisdiction, and reputational consequences that affect global operations. For regulated entities — banks, insurance companies, investment firms — a criminal conviction in Spain can have cascading regulatory consequences across multiple jurisdictions.

Fourth, the interaction with other national corporate liability regimes. A multinational facing parallel investigations in Spain and another jurisdiction (such as the United Kingdom under the Bribery Act or the United States under the FCPA) must coordinate its defence across legal systems with different substantive and procedural requirements. The compliance defence that succeeds in Spain may not apply in another jurisdiction, and vice versa.
