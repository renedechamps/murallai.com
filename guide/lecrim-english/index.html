LECrim Explained: Spain's Criminal Procedure Law in Plain English

The 1882 code that still governs every criminal case in Spain, decoded for international practitioners

Last updated: May 2026 Reading time: ~18 min 3,500+ words
TL;DR — The Ley de Enjuiciamiento Criminal (LECrim) is Spain's criminal procedure code, enacted in 1882 and still in force with extensive amendments. It governs every stage of criminal proceedings, from investigation through trial and appeals. International lawyers need to understand key provisions including Art. 118 (rights of the investigado), Art. 520 (detention rights), Art. 787 (conformidad in the procedimiento abreviado), and the appeals framework. A comprehensive reform bill has been debated for years but the current text, despite its age, remains the governing statute.

What Is the LECrim and Why Does It Still Matter?

The Ley de Enjuiciamiento Criminal (LECrim), formally designated as the Royal Decree of 14 September 1882 approving the Law of Criminal Procedure, is the statute that governs criminal proceedings in Spain. International lawyers encountering the Spanish criminal justice system for the first time are often surprised to learn that the code in force predates the telephone exchange in Madrid.

Despite its age, the LECrim is far from a historical artifact. It has been amended dozens of times since its enactment, with particularly significant reforms in 2002 (introducing the enjuiciamiento rápido, or fast-track procedure), 2015 (time limits for investigations, expanded appeals, enhanced defendant rights transposing EU Directives), and subsequent years addressing digital evidence, technological surveillance measures, and European judicial cooperation.

The LECrim operates alongside several other statutes that are essential for a complete understanding of Spanish criminal law: the Código Penal (Criminal Code, Organic Law 10/1995), the Ley Orgánica del Poder Judicial (Organic Law of the Judiciary), the Ley del Tribunal del Jurado (Jury Court Law, Organic Law 5/1995), and the Estatuto de la Víctima del Delito (Victims' Rights Statute, Law 4/2015), among others.

For years, successive Spanish governments have proposed a comprehensive overhaul of the LECrim, including proposals to shift the direction of investigations from the juez de instrucción to the Ministerio Fiscal (a model closer to the German or Italian systems). As of May 2026, these reform proposals remain under debate, and the current text continues to govern all criminal proceedings in Spain.

How Is the LECrim Structured?

The LECrim is organized into seven Books (Libros), each covering a distinct area of criminal procedure. Understanding this structure helps international lawyers locate relevant provisions efficiently.

Book Subject Matter Key Provisions
Book I General provisions Jurisdiction, parties, procedural capacity, recusal, legal aid
Book II The sumario (investigation in procedimiento ordinario) Initiation, investigative measures, provisional measures, secreto de sumario
Book III The juicio oral (oral trial) Trial procedure, evidence, conformidad (Art. 655), judgment
Book IV Special procedures Proceedings against senators/deputies, jury trials, procedimiento abreviado, enjuiciamiento rápido, delitos leves
Book V Appeals (recursos) Recurso de reforma, apelación, casación, extraordinary review
Book VI Enforcement of judgments Execution of sentences, suspensión (suspension), sustitución (substitution)
Book VII Costs and final provisions Costas procesales (procedural costs), transitional and final provisions

In practice, international lawyers will spend most of their time with Books II and IV (investigation procedures), Book III (trial), and Book V (appeals). The procedimiento abreviado, found in Title II of Book IV, is the most commonly used procedural track.

Which Articles of the LECrim Should Every International Lawyer Know?

While the LECrim contains hundreds of articles, certain provisions are so frequently invoked that familiarity with them is essential for any international lawyer managing a Spanish criminal matter.

Art. 118 LECrim — Rights of the Investigado

Art. 118, substantially reformed by Organic Law 13/2015, establishes the rights that attach to any person from the moment they are attributed criminal conduct, whether formally or informally. These include: the right to be informed of the facts and rights, the right to examine the proceedings, the right to counsel (and to free legal assistance if eligible), the right to an interpreter if needed, and the right to remain silent. Art. 118 is the gateway provision for defendant rights during the instrucción and must be complied with from the very first police or judicial contact with the suspect.

Art. 520 LECrim — Rights of Detained Persons

Art. 520 sets out the detailed rights of any person who is detained (detenido). It requires that the detainee be informed, in language they understand, of the facts motivating the detention, the rights available (including silence, counsel, medical examination, interpreter, consular notification for foreign nationals, and communication with family), and the maximum duration of detention. Detention without judicial authorization may not exceed 72 hours (Art. 17.2 of the Spanish Constitution), and the detainee must be brought before a judge or released within that period. For international clients detained in Spain, Art. 520 compliance is the first issue to verify.

Art. 655 LECrim — Conformidad in the Procedimiento Ordinario

Art. 655 governs conformidad (plea agreement) in the procedimiento ordinario. When the defence brief (escrito de calificación provisional de la defensa) is filed, the defendant may accept the most serious charge and requested sentence as stated by the prosecution. If the court considers the conformidad legally sound and the defendant has given informed consent, the court proceeds directly to judgment without a full trial. This provision is available only when the sentence requested does not exceed six years' imprisonment.

Art. 787 LECrim — Conformidad in the Procedimiento Abreviado

Art. 787 is the most practically important conformidad provision, applicable to the procedimiento abreviado (the most common procedural track). It allows the defendant to accept the accusation and the requested sentence at any point before the trial begins, or at the very opening of the trial. The agreed sentence must not exceed six years' imprisonment. Unlike Art. 655, Art. 787 permits negotiation between the parties on the specific charge and sentence, making it functionally closer (though not identical) to common-law plea bargaining. The court must verify that the defendant understands the consequences, that the defence lawyer agrees, and that the facts support the legal classification.

Art. 801 LECrim — Fast-Track Conformidad

Art. 801, introduced by the 2002 reform creating the enjuiciamiento rápido (fast-track procedure), provides an additional incentive for early resolution: if the defendant reaches conformidad before the guardia court (the court on duty), the sentence can be reduced by up to one-third. This provision is frequently used for straightforward cases, particularly those involving offences detected in flagrante such as drink-driving, minor assaults, and theft. It represents the most lenient sentencing outcome available through procedural agreement in Spanish law.

What Are the Limitation Periods for Criminal Offences in Spain?

Limitation periods (prescripción del delito) are governed not by the LECrim but by Articles 131-132 of the Código Penal (Criminal Code). However, any international lawyer working on a Spanish criminal matter must understand these rules, as they determine whether prosecution is time-barred.

Maximum Sentence for the Offence Limitation Period
More than 15 years' imprisonment 20 years
More than 10 and up to 15 years 15 years
More than 5 and up to 10 years 10 years
More than 3 and up to 5 years 5 years
Up to 3 years 3 years (if imprisonment) or specific rules for lesser penalties
Delitos leves (minor offences) 1 year
Genocide, crimes against humanity Imprescriptible (no limitation)

The limitation period begins to run from the date the offence was committed (dies a quo). For continuing offences (delito continuado), it runs from the date of the last act. For offences of omission, it runs from the date the obligation should have been fulfilled.

Under Art. 132 of the Código Penal, the limitation period is interrupted when judicial proceedings are directed against the person (dirigir el procedimiento contra el culpable). The Tribunal Supremo has interpreted this requirement strictly: a mere denuncia or police investigation is generally insufficient; the proceedings must be judicially directed against a specific identified person. When the limitation period is interrupted, a new period of equal duration begins from the date of the last procedural act directed against the suspect.

For international firms, limitation periods are frequently relevant in cases involving historical fraud, tax offences, or corporate misconduct. Spain's tax offences under Art. 305 of the Código Penal carry a maximum penalty of five years' imprisonment (or six years in aggravated cases), corresponding to a five-year limitation period. However, the interaction between the criminal limitation period and the separate administrative limitation period for tax assessment (four years under Art. 66 of the Ley General Tributaria) can create complex timing issues.

How Does Prisión Provisional (Pretrial Detention) Work?

Prisión provisional (pretrial detention or remand in custody) is the most severe provisional measure available in Spanish criminal proceedings. It is governed by Articles 502-519 of the LECrim and must comply with the constitutional right to liberty under Art. 17 of the Spanish Constitution and Art. 5 of the European Convention on Human Rights.

Prisión provisional may only be ordered by a judge and requires:

  • The existence of one or more offences punishable by at least two years' imprisonment (or a lesser penalty in specific circumstances such as habitual offenders with outstanding arrest warrants)
  • Sufficient evidence linking the person to the alleged offence (indicios racionales de criminalidad)
  • The presence of a specific ground justifying detention: risk of absconding (riesgo de fuga), risk of evidence destruction (riesgo de destrucción de pruebas), risk of reoffending (riesgo de reiteración delictiva), or risk to the victim's safety

The maximum duration of prisión provisional depends on the applicable sentence and the grounds for detention. For offences carrying more than three years' imprisonment: one year, extendable to two years by reasoned judicial decision. For offences carrying three years or less: six months, extendable to one year. When the sole ground is risk of absconding and the applicable sentence exceeds three years, the maximum is two years, extendable to four years. These limits are absolute and constitutional; exceeding them requires immediate release.

International firms should note that alternatives to prisión provisional include libertad provisional (conditional release) with conditions such as comparecencia periódica (periodic reporting to the court), passport surrender, and fianza (bail/bond). The bail system in Spain operates differently from common law: fianza is typically a monetary guarantee against civil liability rather than a condition for release per se, although it can serve both functions.

How Do Appeals (Recursos) Work Under the LECrim?

The Spanish appeals system in criminal matters has been significantly reformed to comply with the right to a double instance (second review on facts and law), mandated by Art. 14(5) of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights and developed through the jurisprudence of the Tribunal Constitucional.

Recurso de Reforma (Reconsideration)

The recurso de reforma is a request for the same court or judge that issued a decision to reconsider it. It is available against interlocutory orders (autos) and procedural decisions during the instrucción. Filing a recurso de reforma is often a prerequisite for filing an apelación. It is resolved by the same judge, typically within three days, and has a relatively low success rate. Nonetheless, it is procedurally important because failure to file it can preclude subsequent remedies.

Recurso de Apelación (Appeal)

The recurso de apelación is the primary appellate remedy. Following the 2015 reform (Organic Law 41/2015), a full appeal on facts and law is available against first-instance criminal judgments. Appeals against judgments of the Juzgado de lo Penal (Criminal Court) are heard by the Audiencia Provincial (Provincial Court). Appeals against judgments of the Audiencia Provincial (sitting as a first-instance trial court) are heard by the Sala de lo Civil y Penal del Tribunal Superior de Justicia (Civil and Criminal Chamber of the Superior Court of Justice of the relevant autonomous community).

The apelación allows review of the legal classification of facts, the assessment of evidence (within limits), the proportionality of the sentence, and procedural regularity. The appellate court can acquit, reduce the sentence, or order a retrial. It can also increase the sentence if the prosecution has appealed, but only within the bounds of the accusation.

Recurso de Casación (Cassation)

The recurso de casación before the Tribunal Supremo (Supreme Court) is the highest ordinary remedy. It is available on three grounds: (1) infracción de ley (violation of substantive law), (2) quebrantamiento de forma (procedural defect), and (3) violation of constitutional rights (principally under Art. 24 of the Constitution). Casación is not a third instance: the Tribunal Supremo reviews legal questions but does not generally reassess the factual findings made by the trial and appellate courts.

Following the 2015 reform, the Tribunal Supremo has expanded access to casación for cases involving important legal questions, even where the sentences imposed are relatively low. This has made casación a more significant tool for ensuring uniform interpretation of criminal law across Spain.

Recurso de Amparo (Constitutional Appeal)

Beyond the ordinary appeal system, parties whose fundamental rights have been violated may file a recurso de amparo (constitutional protection appeal) before the Tribunal Constitucional (Constitutional Court). This is an extraordinary remedy available only after exhausting all ordinary appeals. The Tribunal Constitucional can declare the violation and nullify the relevant judicial decision, requiring the courts to issue a new one respecting the fundamental right in question. The amparo has been decisive in developing Spanish criminal procedural guarantees, particularly regarding the presumption of innocence, the right to a fair trial, and the exclusionary rule.

What Recent Reforms and Trends Are Shaping the LECrim?

Several developments are reshaping the landscape of Spanish criminal procedure, even in the absence of the long-anticipated comprehensive reform:

Digital Evidence and Technology

Organic Law 13/2015 introduced detailed provisions for digital investigative measures, including the interception of electronic communications, access to computer data, remote registration of equipment (registro remoto de equipos informáticos), and use of technical devices for geolocation. These provisions, found in Articles 588 bis a through 588 octies of the LECrim, require prior judicial authorization and are subject to principles of proportionality, necessity, and specificity. For international firms handling cases involving digital evidence, understanding these provisions and their judicial authorization requirements is essential.

Corporate Criminal Liability

While corporate criminal liability is substantively governed by Art. 31 bis of the Código Penal, its procedural implementation raises LECrim questions regarding representation of the legal entity, the relationship between the company's procedural position and that of individual co-defendants, and the application of conformidad to corporate defendants. The Fiscalía General del Estado has issued Circular 1/2011 and Circular 1/2016 providing guidance on these procedural aspects.

European Judicial Cooperation

The LECrim's provisions on international judicial cooperation have been substantially supplemented by Law 23/2014 on mutual recognition of criminal judicial decisions in the European Union, which implements the European Arrest Warrant, the European Investigation Order, and other EU instruments. These provisions interact with and, in the context of EU cooperation, effectively supersede the LECrim's traditional provisions on international comisiones rogatorias (letters rogatory).

Victim Protection Enhancements

The Estatuto de la Víctima del Delito (Law 4/2015) and subsequent reforms have strengthened victim participation rights, introduced individual needs assessments for vulnerable victims, and expanded protective measures during proceedings. These reforms operate alongside the LECrim and have practical implications for how proceedings are conducted, particularly regarding victim testimony and confrontation with the accused.

Efficiency Measures and Backlog Reduction

Spanish courts face significant backlogs, and successive reforms have sought to improve efficiency through time limits on investigations, promotion of conformidad, fast-track procedures, and specialization of courts. The creation of specialized juzgados de violencia sobre la mujer (gender violence courts) and the increasing use of digital case management systems reflect this trend. For international practitioners, the practical reality of court backlogs and processing times must be factored into any case timeline.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the LECrim and why is it still relevant?

The Ley de Enjuiciamiento Criminal (LECrim) is Spain's criminal procedure code, originally enacted in 1882. Despite its age, it remains the primary statute governing criminal proceedings in Spain and has been extensively amended, most recently through significant reforms in 2015. It governs everything from initiation of proceedings through investigation, trial, and appeals. A comprehensive replacement has been debated for years but has not been enacted.

What are the limitation periods for criminal offences in Spain?

Limitation periods (prescripción) are set by Articles 131-132 of the Código Penal. They range from one year for delitos leves (minor offences) to 20 years for offences carrying more than 15 years' imprisonment. Genocide and crimes against humanity have no limitation period. The clock runs from the date of commission and is interrupted when judicial proceedings are directed against the suspect.

How does conformidad differ from a common-law guilty plea?

Conformidad shares the function of resolving cases without a full trial, but it is structurally distinct. It requires the agreement of the defendant, their lawyer, and the prosecution. Under Art. 787 LECrim, the agreed sentence cannot exceed six years' imprisonment. Unlike common-law plea bargaining, the court can reject the conformidad if it considers the facts insufficient or the sentence disproportionate. The defendant must give informed consent, and the defence lawyer must explicitly agree.

What appeals are available in Spanish criminal cases?

The main remedies are: recurso de reforma (reconsideration by the same judge), recurso de apelación (appeal on facts and law to a higher court), and recurso de casación (cassation before the Tribunal Supremo on grounds of legal error, procedural defect, or constitutional violation). An extraordinary recurso de amparo before the Tribunal Constitucional is available for fundamental rights violations after exhausting ordinary appeals.
