The Complete Guide to Spanish Criminal Procedure for International Lawyers

How criminal cases move through Spain's courts, explained for common-law practitioners

Last updated: May 2026 Reading time: ~20 min 4,000+ words
TL;DR — Spanish criminal procedure is an inquisitorial system structured around a juez de instrucción (investigating judge) who directs the investigation, a separate trial judge or panel, and a powerful public prosecutor (Ministerio Fiscal). Proceedings move through five phases: initiation, judicial investigation (instrucción), an intermediate screening phase, an oral trial (juicio oral), and judgment with appeals. Victims can join the prosecution as acusación particular. Understanding these structural differences is essential for any international firm advising clients exposed to Spanish criminal jurisdiction.

Why Does Spanish Criminal Procedure Matter to Your Firm?

If your client is a multinational corporation, a senior executive, or a high-net-worth individual with exposure to Spain, you cannot rely on assumptions borrowed from English, American, or other common-law criminal systems. Spanish criminal procedure operates on fundamentally different principles, inherited from the continental European tradition and codified primarily in the Ley de Enjuiciamiento Criminal (LECrim) of 1882, as extensively amended.

Spain is the fourth-largest economy in the eurozone and a major hub for cross-border investment, tourism, and real estate. Criminal exposure for international clients arises in contexts including corporate fraud, tax offences, money laundering, environmental crimes, labor law violations, and increasingly, cybercrime. Whether your client is an executive facing a querella (criminal complaint with party status), a company subject to corporate criminal liability under Art. 31 bis of the Código Penal (Criminal Code), or a British national facing extradition, understanding the procedural architecture is not optional.

This guide provides a rigorous, practitioner-oriented overview of the entire procedural framework, written for partners and senior associates at Magic Circle, AmLaw 100, and equivalent international firms. It is not a substitute for local counsel but rather a tool to help you supervise Spanish proceedings, manage client expectations, and make informed strategic decisions.

How Does Spanish Criminal Procedure Differ from Common Law Systems?

The single most important structural difference is the role of the juez de instrucción (investigating judge). In common law jurisdictions, the police and prosecution investigate; in Spain, the investigation is judicially led. The juez de instrucción directs the instrucción (judicial investigation), orders searches, wiretaps, and arrests, and decides whether the case proceeds to trial. This judge does not, however, try the case. Trial is conducted by a different judge or court, ensuring structural separation between investigation and adjudication.

Other critical differences include:

  • No jury trial as default. Most cases are decided by professional judges. The Tribunal del Jurado (jury court) exists but is limited to specific offences such as homicide, threats, arson, and crimes by public officials.
  • Victim as party. The acusación particular (private prosecution by the victim) and acusación popular (prosecution by any citizen) have no direct common-law equivalent. The victim can become a full prosecuting party with rights to propose evidence and request specific sentences.
  • Written proceedings coexist with oral hearings. While the juicio oral is indeed oral and governed by principles of immediacy and contradiction, the instrucción is largely written and documentary.
  • No discovery as common law understands it. There is no pre-trial disclosure regime comparable to CPR Part 31 or FRCP Rules 26-37. Evidence is gathered by or under the authority of the juez de instrucción, and the parties request specific investigative measures.
  • Double jeopardy applies but with nuances. The principle of ne bis in idem (prohibition of double jeopardy) is constitutionally recognized, but its interaction with administrative sanctions and with proceedings in other jurisdictions can raise complex questions.

What Are the Phases of Spanish Criminal Procedure?

Spanish criminal procedure moves through five sequential phases. Understanding each phase, including its purpose, the key actors involved, and the decisions that shape the trajectory of the case, is essential for effective case management.

Phase 1: Initiation — Denuncia and Querella

Criminal proceedings in Spain are initiated through one of several mechanisms. The most common are the denuncia (report of a crime) and the querella (formal criminal complaint conferring party status on the complainant).

A denuncia is a simple notification to the authorities that a crime may have been committed. Any person can file a denuncia, and in certain cases there is a legal obligation to do so (Art. 259 LECrim). Filing a denuncia does not make the filer a party to the proceedings.

A querella, by contrast, is a formal complaint filed before the competent juzgado de instrucción (investigating court) through a procurador (court representative) and signed by an abogado (lawyer). The querellante becomes the acusación particular, acquiring full party status with the right to participate in every phase of the proceedings. For international clients who are victims of crime in Spain, filing a querella is often strategically preferable because it provides procedural control.

Proceedings may also be initiated de oficio (by the court's own initiative) or by the Ministerio Fiscal (Public Prosecutor's Office, commonly known as the Fiscalía).

Phase 2: Instrucción (Judicial Investigation)

The instrucción is the core investigative phase, directed by the juez de instrucción. Its purpose is to determine whether sufficient evidence exists to justify proceeding to trial and to preserve evidence that might otherwise be lost.

During the instrucción, the judge may order a wide range of investigative measures: witness statements (declaraciones testificales), expert reports (periciales), searches (registros domiciliarios), telecommunications interceptions (intervenciones telefónicas), bank account investigations, seizure of documents, and identification parades (ruedas de reconocimiento). The parties may request that the judge adopt specific measures, but the judge decides.

The person under investigation is termed the investigado (investigated person) at this stage. Under Art. 118 LECrim, the investigado has the right to be informed of the facts attributed to them, to be assisted by counsel, to examine the proceedings (subject to any secreto de sumario, or investigative secrecy), and to propose investigative measures.

The instrucción is subject to time limits introduced by the 2015 reform: as a general rule, 12 months for simple cases and 18 months for complex ones (causas complejas), extendable by judicial decision with reasons.

Phase 3: Fase Intermedia (Intermediate Phase)

When the juez de instrucción considers the investigation complete, the proceedings enter the fase intermedia (intermediate phase). The purpose of this phase is to determine whether the case should proceed to juicio oral or be dismissed.

In the procedimiento abreviado (shortened procedure, the most common track), the judge issues an auto de transformación (order converting the case to the abreviado track), and the Ministerio Fiscal and other accusatory parties file their escritos de acusación (indictment briefs). The defence then files its escrito de defensa (defence brief).

If the judge determines there is sufficient basis, they issue the auto de apertura de juicio oral (order opening the oral trial). This decision transfers the case to the trial court. If the judge concludes there is insufficient evidence, they issue an auto de sobreseimiento (dismissal order), which may be libre (definitive, equivalent to an acquittal) or provisional (allowing reopening if new evidence emerges).

Phase 4: Juicio Oral (Oral Trial)

The juicio oral (oral hearing) is governed by principles of oralidad (orality), inmediación (immediacy), contradicción (adversarial challenge), and publicidad (openness). It is conducted before a different judge or panel from the one who directed the instrucción.

The trial follows a structured sequence: preliminary questions (cuestiones previas), prosecution's case, defence case, examination of evidence (documentary, witness, and expert), oral conclusions (conclusiones definitivas), and the defendant's right to have the last word (derecho a la última palabra).

Only evidence produced or ratified at trial, with full adversarial guarantees, has probative value for the judgment. Evidence from the instrucción is generally not sufficient on its own unless it was conducted under conditions equivalent to trial (pre-constituted evidence, or prueba preconstituida).

Phase 5: Sentencia and Recursos (Judgment and Appeals)

The court issues a sentencia (judgment) within the legally prescribed timeframe. The sentencia must contain a detailed statement of proven facts (hechos probados), the legal reasoning (fundamentos de derecho), and the operative part (fallo) including the sentence or acquittal.

Parties dissatisfied with the judgment may exercise various recursos (appeals). The primary appellate remedy is recurso de apelación (appeal), heard by the Audiencia Provincial (Provincial Court) or the corresponding higher court. Beyond that, the recurso de casación (cassation appeal) before the Tribunal Supremo (Supreme Court) is available on grounds of legal error, procedural defect, or constitutional violation. The Spanish appeals system was significantly expanded by the 2015 reform to guarantee the right to a second instance (double review) in criminal cases, in line with Art. 14(5) of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights.

Who Are the Key Actors in Spanish Criminal Proceedings?

Juez de Instrucción (Investigating Judge)

The juez de instrucción directs the investigation, orders measures affecting fundamental rights (such as detention, searches, and wiretaps), and decides whether to send the case to trial. The judge is bound by the principle of legality and must investigate both incriminating and exculpatory evidence. In practice, the quality of the instrucción varies significantly between courts, and the relationship between the judge, the Fiscalía, and the parties is central to the dynamics of the case.

Ministerio Fiscal (Public Prosecutor's Office)

The Ministerio Fiscal, commonly referred to as the Fiscalía, is constitutionally mandated to promote the action of justice in defence of legality, the rights of citizens, and the public interest (Art. 124 of the Spanish Constitution). The Fiscalía participates in the instrucción, files the accusation brief, and presents the prosecution at trial. Unlike in many common law systems, the Fiscalía does not direct the investigation (that role belongs to the judge) but does exercise significant influence. The Fiscal General del Estado (Attorney General) heads the institution.

Acusación Particular (Private Prosecutor)

The acusación particular is the victim (or their legal successor) exercising the right to prosecute. This figure has full party status: the right to propose evidence, make legal arguments, request specific sentences, and appeal. In cases involving international clients as victims, the acusación particular is the primary mechanism for ensuring active participation in the proceedings.

Acusación Popular (People's Prosecution)

Under Art. 125 of the Spanish Constitution and Art. 101 LECrim, any Spanish citizen (and, subject to conditions, EU citizens and certain legal persons) may exercise the acción popular (people's prosecution action) to prosecute crimes, even without being a victim. This unusual institution occasionally surfaces in high-profile cases where interest groups or political parties join the prosecution.

Letrado de la Administración de Justicia (Court Clerk)

The Letrado de la Administración de Justicia (LAJ, formerly known as Secretario Judicial) is a legally qualified court official who manages procedural aspects of the case, authenticates judicial acts, issues notifications, and has decision-making authority over certain procedural matters. International practitioners frequently underestimate the role of the LAJ, who can be a critical point of contact for procedural queries.

What Are the Different Types of Criminal Procedure in Spain?

The LECrim establishes several procedural tracks, each tailored to different categories of offences based on their severity. The choice of procedure has significant implications for timelines, procedural rights, and available mechanisms such as conformidad (plea agreement).

Procedure Applicable Offences Key Features
Procedimiento ordinario (ordinary procedure) Offences punishable by more than 9 years imprisonment Full sumario (formal investigation); tried by the Audiencia Provincial (three-judge panel)
Procedimiento abreviado (shortened procedure) Offences punishable by up to 9 years imprisonment Simplified investigation (diligencias previas); most common procedural track in practice
Enjuiciamiento rápido (fast-track procedure) Specific offences detected in flagrante or with straightforward investigation Accelerated timeline; conformidad with sentence reduction of up to one-third (Art. 801 LECrim)
Juicio de delitos leves (minor offences trial) Delitos leves (minor offences, formerly faltas) Summary oral hearing; no formal investigation phase; immediate judgment
Tribunal del Jurado (jury procedure) Specific offences (homicide, threats, bribery by officials, etc.) Nine-member citizen jury; Magistrado-Presidente presides

The procedimiento abreviado is by far the most frequently used track and the one that international practitioners will encounter most often. It applies to all offences carrying a maximum penalty of nine years or less, which encompasses the vast majority of white-collar crimes relevant to international practice: fraud (estafa), tax offences (delitos fiscales), money laundering (blanqueo de capitales), corruption, and corporate criminal liability matters.

How Do Evidence Rules Work in Spanish Criminal Proceedings?

The Spanish law of criminal evidence operates on the principle that only evidence produced at the juicio oral, with full adversarial guarantees, can support a conviction. This rule, established by the Tribunal Constitucional (Constitutional Court) and the Tribunal Supremo, means that the evidence gathered during the instrucción is, in principle, mere investigative material that must be reproduced or ratified at trial.

Types of Evidence

  • Prueba documental (documentary evidence): Includes public and private documents, business records, electronic evidence, expert reports, and official certificates. Documentary evidence is read aloud or displayed at trial and can be challenged by the parties.
  • Prueba testifical (witness evidence): Witnesses testify orally at trial and are subject to examination and cross-examination. Witness statements made during the instrucción can be introduced at trial under specific conditions (Art. 714 and 730 LECrim) but are not automatically probative.
  • Prueba pericial (expert evidence): Expert witnesses (peritos) present their reports and are examined at trial. Both parties may appoint their own experts, and the court may appoint official experts. Expert evidence on financial, accounting, digital forensic, and technical matters is increasingly central in white-collar cases.
  • Defendant's statement: The acusado (defendant at trial) has the right to testify or to remain silent. Silence cannot be used as evidence of guilt, consistent with the derecho a no declarar contra sí mismo (privilege against self-incrimination) under Art. 24.2 of the Spanish Constitution.

Exclusionary Rules

Evidence obtained in violation of fundamental rights is null and void and must be excluded, together with any derivative evidence tainted by the original violation. This is the doctrine of the árbol envenenado (fruit of the poisonous tree), recognized by the Tribunal Constitucional in its landmark STC 114/1984. The exclusionary rule covers, among others, unlawful searches, unauthorized wiretaps, violations of the right to counsel, and coerced confessions. Spanish courts have developed a nuanced jurisprudence on the scope of the exclusionary rule, including exceptions for independent source, inevitable discovery, and attenuated connection.

What Rights Does the Defendant Have in Spanish Criminal Proceedings?

The rights of the defendant are anchored in Art. 24 of the Spanish Constitution (right to effective judicial protection and due process) and detailed in the LECrim, particularly after the significant reforms transposing EU Directives on procedural rights.

  • Right to information: The investigado must be informed of the facts attributed, the rights available, and the identity of the accuser, from the earliest moment (Art. 118 LECrim).
  • Right to counsel: The derecho a la asistencia letrada (right to legal assistance) applies from the moment of detention or attribution of criminal conduct. If the person cannot afford counsel, a publicly funded abogado de oficio (duty lawyer) is appointed.
  • Right to silence: The investigado has the absolute right to remain silent and not to incriminate themselves.
  • Right to interpretation and translation: Non-Spanish-speaking defendants have the right to an interpreter at all stages and to translation of essential documents (transposing Directive 2010/64/EU).
  • Presumption of innocence: The presunción de inocencia is a fundamental right under Art. 24.2 of the Constitution and can only be overcome by evidence produced at trial with full guarantees.
  • Right to examine the file: Subject to any secreto de sumario declared by the judge, the investigado and their lawyer have the right to access the case file (acceso al atestado y a las actuaciones).
  • Habeas corpus: Any person unlawfully detained has the right to seek immediate judicial review under Organic Law 6/1984 on habeas corpus.

For international clients, particular attention should be paid to the rights of detained persons under Art. 520 LECrim, which establishes detailed requirements for information, legal assistance, medical examination, consular notification (for foreign nationals), and communication with family members. Failure to comply with these requirements can render evidence inadmissible.

What Is the Role of the Víctima and Acusación Particular?

Spain has one of the most developed frameworks for victim participation in criminal proceedings in Europe. The Estatuto de la Víctima del Delito (Victims' Rights Statute, Law 4/2015) consolidated and expanded the rights of victims, transposing EU Directive 2012/29/EU.

Any victim of a crime may constitute themselves as acusación particular by filing a querella or by joining existing proceedings through a personación (formal appearance) at any point during the instrucción. Once constituted, the acusación particular has substantially the same procedural rights as the Ministerio Fiscal:

  • Right to access the full case file
  • Right to request investigative measures from the juez de instrucción
  • Right to file an independent accusation brief, potentially seeking a different classification of the crime or a different sentence from the Fiscalía
  • Right to propose and challenge evidence at trial
  • Right to make oral conclusions at trial
  • Right to appeal the judgment

Importantly, proceedings can continue with the acusación particular alone even if the Ministerio Fiscal requests dismissal. This is a powerful tool for victims whose interests may not be fully aligned with those of the public prosecutor. For international firms representing corporate victims of fraud or other crimes in Spain, the acusación particular is the standard mechanism for active case management.

What Practical Tips Should International Firms Keep in Mind?

Based on extensive experience advising international firms in Spanish criminal matters, the following practical considerations are consistently relevant:

  1. Appoint Spanish counsel early. The instrucción is where the case is shaped. Waiting until trial to engage local counsel means losing the opportunity to influence the investigative phase. Spanish criminal proceedings require representation by both an abogado and a procurador.
  2. Understand the procurador system. The procurador de los tribunales is a mandatory procedural representative who handles notifications, filings, and court communications. This is a separate figure from the abogado. International lawyers unfamiliar with this dual-representation requirement sometimes discover it too late.
  3. Do not assume Anglo-American timelines. Spanish proceedings are often protracted. A complex white-collar instrucción can last years despite statutory time limits. Plan accordingly and manage client expectations from the outset.
  4. Consider conformidad strategically. The Spanish conformidad (plea agreement) under Art. 787 LECrim (for the procedimiento abreviado) and Art. 801 LECrim (for enjuiciamiento rápido) can produce significantly reduced sentences, but the timing and negotiation dynamics differ substantially from common-law plea bargaining.
  5. Preserve privilege carefully. Spanish law recognizes secreto profesional (professional secrecy) of lawyers under Art. 542 of the Ley Orgánica del Poder Judicial, but the scope and enforcement mechanisms differ from common-law legal professional privilege. There is no exact equivalent of litigation privilege as understood in English law.
  6. Monitor civil liability within criminal proceedings. Spanish criminal proceedings routinely include claims for responsabilidad civil (civil liability / damages). The acusación particular or the Fiscalía will typically claim civil damages within the criminal case, and the judgment will include a ruling on compensation. This is procedurally efficient but requires attention to the civil dimension from the outset.
  7. Coordinate with regulatory proceedings. Many offences relevant to international practice, particularly in the financial sector, involve parallel regulatory investigations by bodies such as the Comisión Nacional del Mercado de Valores (CNMV), Banco de España, or Agencia Tributaria. The interaction between criminal and administrative proceedings raises issues of ne bis in idem, compelled testimony, and document disclosure that require careful coordination.
  8. Factor in corporate criminal liability. Since the 2010 reform (amended in 2015), Spanish law recognizes corporate criminal liability under Art. 31 bis of the Código Penal. Companies can be fined, dissolved, or barred from public contracts. The existence and effectiveness of a programa de cumplimiento (compliance program) is a statutory defence. International firms should assess their clients' compliance frameworks against the Spanish legal standards, which are detailed in the Circular 1/2016 de la Fiscalía General del Estado.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the main phases of Spanish criminal procedure?

Spanish criminal procedure consists of five main phases: (1) initiation through denuncia or querella, (2) instrucción (judicial investigation) led by the juez de instrucción, (3) fase intermedia (intermediate phase) where the case is assessed for trial, (4) juicio oral (oral hearing), and (5) sentencia (judgment) followed by available recursos (appeals). Each phase has distinct procedural rules, actors, and strategic considerations.

What is the role of the juez de instrucción in Spain?

The juez de instrucción (investigating judge) leads the instrucción phase, directing the investigation, ordering evidence-gathering measures, deciding on provisional measures such as prisión provisional (pretrial detention), and ultimately determining whether sufficient evidence exists to proceed to trial. Unlike common law systems where prosecutors direct investigations, this judicial figure occupies a central, quasi-inquisitorial role. The investigating judge is structurally separate from the trial judge.

Can a victim participate directly in Spanish criminal proceedings?

Yes. Spain allows the víctima (victim) to become an acusación particular (private prosecutor), granting full party status. The acusación particular can request investigative measures, propose evidence, file independent accusation briefs, make legal arguments at trial, and appeal the judgment. Proceedings can even continue on the basis of the acusación particular alone if the Ministerio Fiscal requests dismissal.

How does conformidad (plea bargaining) work in Spain?

Conformidad is Spain's functional equivalent of a guilty plea, though structurally distinct. In the procedimiento abreviado, conformidad under Art. 787 LECrim allows the defendant to accept the most serious accusation (or an agreed-upon reduced charge) provided the sentence does not exceed six years' imprisonment. In the procedimiento ordinario, conformidad under Art. 655 LECrim is available at the opening of juicio oral. The fast-track conformidad under Art. 801 LECrim permits a further sentence reduction of up to one-third. Conformidad requires the agreement of the defendant, the defence lawyer, and the prosecution.
