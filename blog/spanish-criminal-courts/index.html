Spanish Criminal Courts Explained: From Juzgado to Tribunal Supremo

By Muralla AI | Last updated: May 2026 | 15 min read
TL;DR Spain's criminal court system is organised in a multi-tiered hierarchy. Juzgados de Instrucción (investigating courts) handle the pre-trial investigation. Juzgados de lo Penal (criminal courts) try offences with sentences up to five years. Audiencias Provinciales (provincial courts) try more serious offences. The Audiencia Nacional (National Court) handles terrorism, large-scale economic crime, and other nationally significant cases. The Tribunal Supremo (Supreme Court) hears cassation appeals. The Tribunal Constitucional (Constitutional Court) adjudicates fundamental rights claims. Understanding which court handles which case is essential for international firms advising clients in Spain.

For international lawyers encountering the Spanish criminal justice system for the first time, the court structure presents an immediate challenge. Unlike the relatively straightforward division between magistrates' courts and Crown courts in England, or the federal-state distinction in the United States, Spain's criminal court hierarchy reflects a complex combination of territorial organisation, subject-matter jurisdiction, and the constitutional separation between investigative and adjudicative functions.

This guide maps the entire structure, explains the jurisdictional rules that determine which court handles which case, and identifies the practical implications that international firms need to understand.

What Are Juzgados de Instruccion and What Do They Do?

The Juzgados de Instrucción (investigating courts) form the foundation of Spain's criminal court system. Every criminal case in Spain begins with an investigation conducted by a Juez de Instrucción (investigating judge). This is the most distinctive feature of the Spanish system for common law practitioners: the investigation is a judicial function, not a prosecutorial one.

The investigating judge's role encompasses several critical functions:

  • Receiving complaints and reports: Criminal proceedings may be initiated by a denuncia (complaint) from anyone with knowledge of a potential offence, a querella (formal accusation) filed by the victim or the Ministerio Fiscal (prosecution), or a direct police report (atestado).
  • Directing the investigation: The investigating judge decides what evidence to gather, which witnesses to summon, and what investigative measures to authorise. The police act under the judge's direction during the judicial investigation phase, though they retain autonomous powers during the initial stages of a case.
  • Authorising intrusive measures: Wiretaps, search warrants, bank account seizures, and other measures that affect fundamental rights require judicial authorisation. The investigating judge issues these orders, subject to constitutional requirements of proportionality and necessity.
  • Deciding on pre-trial detention: The investigating judge determines whether the accused should be held in prisión provisional (provisional imprisonment) or released on libertad provisional (conditional release), potentially subject to conditions such as passport surrender or periodic court appearances.
  • Closing the investigation: Once the investigation is complete, the judge either issues an auto de sobreseimiento (dismissal order) if the evidence is insufficient, or an auto de apertura de juicio oral (order opening the oral hearing) if the case should proceed to trial.

There are approximately 400 Juzgados de Instrucción across Spain, organised by territorial district (partido judicial). In larger cities like Madrid and Barcelona, they are numbered sequentially — Juzgado de Instrucción No. 1, No. 2, and so on — with cases assigned by rotation. For international practitioners, identifying which specific Juzgado de Instrucción has a case is the first step in any engagement with the Spanish system.

A critical constitutional principle governs these courts: the judge who investigates can never be the judge who tries the case. This separation, mandated by the Tribunal Constitucional (Constitutional Court) in landmark rulings, ensures that the adjudicating judge approaches the trial without the predispositions that might result from having directed the investigation.

What Are Juzgados de lo Penal and Which Cases Do They Handle?

The Juzgados de lo Penal (criminal courts) are the primary trial courts for less serious criminal offences. Their jurisdiction is defined by a clear sentencing threshold: they conduct the juicio oral (oral hearing) and issue verdicts for offences carrying a maximum sentence of up to five years' imprisonment, or penalties of a different nature — such as fines or community service — regardless of their duration.

These courts are presided over by a single professional judge. There is no jury at this level. The judge hears the evidence, evaluates it according to the principle of libre valoración de la prueba (free evaluation of evidence), and issues a reasoned written judgment (sentencia).

The range of cases heard by Juzgados de lo Penal is broad and includes many of the offence types most commonly encountered by international clients: minor assaults, theft, drink driving, less serious fraud, property damage, and a wide variety of other mid-range criminal matters. For firms advising expatriates, tourists, or international business operators who encounter the Spanish criminal system, these courts will be the most frequent point of contact.

Appeals from Juzgados de lo Penal lie to the Audiencia Provincial of the corresponding province. The appeal — known as recurso de apelación (appeal) — allows review of both factual findings and legal determinations, a broader scope than the cassation appeal available at higher levels.

What Are Audiencias Provinciales and When Do They Act as Trial Courts?

The Audiencias Provinciales (provincial courts) occupy a dual position in Spain's criminal court structure. They serve both as trial courts for serious offences and as appellate courts for decisions of the Juzgados de lo Penal within their province.

As Trial Courts

The criminal section (Sección Penal) of an Audiencia Provincial conducts the juicio oral for offences carrying sentences of more than five years' imprisonment. These hearings are conducted before a panel of three professional judges (magistrados), not a single judge. The collegiate composition provides an additional layer of deliberation and reduces the risk of individual judicial error in the most serious cases.

Cases tried by Audiencias Provinciales include serious assaults, robbery with violence, sexual offences, major drug trafficking, significant fraud, and other offences at the upper end of the criminal spectrum. These are typically the highest-stakes proceedings that international firms will encounter.

As Appellate Courts

The Audiencia Provincial also hears appeals against judgments of the Juzgados de lo Penal within its territorial jurisdiction. The appeal is a genuine review on the merits — not limited to questions of law — and the appellate court may examine the evidence, reassess factual findings, and substitute its own judgment where it considers the lower court erred.

There is one Audiencia Provincial for each of Spain's fifty provinces, based in the provincial capital. Some larger provinces, notably Madrid and Barcelona, have multiple criminal sections to manage their caseload.

What Is the Audiencia Nacional and Which Cases Fall Under Its Jurisdiction?

The Audiencia Nacional (National Court) is Spain's specialist court for criminal matters of national significance. Based in Madrid, it exercises jurisdiction over a defined catalogue of offences that, because of their nature or geographic scope, are considered inappropriate for provincial courts. Its jurisdiction is established by the Ley Orgánica del Poder Judicial (LOPJ) (Organic Law of the Judiciary).

The Audiencia Nacional has exclusive criminal jurisdiction over:

  • Terrorism offences: All offences committed by or connected to terrorist organisations are investigated and tried by the Audiencia Nacional. This includes financing of terrorism and glorification of terrorist acts.
  • Large-scale drug trafficking: International narcotics trafficking networks fall within its competence, particularly those operating across multiple Spanish provinces or with significant international connections.
  • Counterfeiting: Currency counterfeiting and related offences affecting the national monetary system.
  • Economic crimes affecting multiple provinces: Fraud, corruption, and other economic offences that have effects across several Spanish provinces and cannot practicably be tried by a single Audiencia Provincial.
  • Offences committed abroad: Criminal acts committed outside Spain that fall under Spanish jurisdiction — whether through the principle of territoriality (effects in Spain), active personality (Spanish perpetrators), or universal jurisdiction — are investigated and tried by the Audiencia Nacional.
  • Crimes against the Crown and government: Offences against members of the Royal Family, government ministers, and other high-ranking officials.

The Audiencia Nacional has its own investigative judges — the Juzgados Centrales de Instrucción (Central Investigating Courts) — who function identically to their provincial counterparts but with national reach. Its trial chamber, the Sala de lo Penal (Criminal Chamber), conducts hearings before a panel of three magistrates.

For international firms, the Audiencia Nacional is the most likely point of contact in matters involving cross-border economic crime, international drug trafficking, or any case with a significant transnational dimension. Its judges are experienced in handling complex multi-jurisdictional matters and are accustomed to dealing with mutual legal assistance requests and international cooperation mechanisms.

What Is the Tribunal Supremo and How Does Cassation Work?

The Tribunal Supremo (Supreme Court), based in Madrid, sits at the apex of Spain's ordinary court system. Its Sala Segunda (Second Chamber), also known as the Sala de lo Penal (Criminal Chamber), is the highest court of appeal in criminal matters.

The Tribunal Supremo exercises its criminal jurisdiction through two primary mechanisms:

Cassation Appeals (Recurso de Casación)

The recurso de casación (cassation appeal) is the standard route to the Tribunal Supremo. Unlike a full appeal, cassation is limited to questions of law. The Tribunal Supremo does not re-examine the facts or reassess the evidence; it reviews whether the lower court correctly applied the substantive criminal law and whether the proceeding complied with procedural requirements and constitutional guarantees.

Cassation is available against judgments of the Audiencias Provinciales, the Audiencia Nacional, and, in limited circumstances, the Tribunales Superiores de Justicia (High Courts of Justice) of the autonomous communities. Common grounds for cassation include misapplication of the criminal law, violation of constitutional rights (particularly the presumption of innocence and the right to effective judicial protection), and procedural irregularities.

First-Instance Jurisdiction

In a limited number of cases, the Tribunal Supremo acts as a first-instance trial court. Under the Spanish Constitution, certain high-ranking officials — including members of the national parliament (Congreso de los Diputados and Senado), government ministers, and the President del Gobierno (Prime Minister) — are subject to the jurisdiction of the Tribunal Supremo for any criminal charges brought against them. This constitutional privilege, known as aforamiento (jurisdictional privilege), ensures that cases against senior political figures are handled at the highest judicial level.

What Role Does the Tribunal Constitucional Play in Criminal Cases?

The Tribunal Constitucional (Constitutional Court) is not part of the ordinary judicial hierarchy — it is a separate institution charged with safeguarding constitutional rights. However, its role in criminal matters is significant through the mechanism of recurso de amparo (constitutional appeal for protection of fundamental rights).

Any party to a criminal proceeding who believes that their fundamental rights — as guaranteed by the Spanish Constitution — have been violated by a court decision may file an amparo appeal with the Tribunal Constitucional. This is available only after all ordinary judicial remedies have been exhausted. Common grounds include violations of the right to a fair trial, the presumption of innocence, the prohibition of indefensión (denial of the right to defence), the right to a reasoned judgment, and the prohibition of double jeopardy.

For international practitioners, the amparo represents a final safeguard. If the ordinary court system — from the trial court through the Tribunal Supremo — has failed to protect a constitutional right, the Tribunal Constitucional provides one last avenue. Its decisions are binding on all courts and, if it finds a violation, it may annul the offending judgment and order a new proceeding.

Beyond the Spanish system, defendants also retain the possibility of application to the Tribunal Europeo de Derechos Humanos (European Court of Human Rights) in Strasbourg, which reviews compliance with the European Convention on Human Rights. This is relevant particularly in cases involving allegations of excessive pre-trial detention, unfair trial procedures, or disproportionate sentencing.

How Do Jurisdiction Rules Work in Practice?

Determining which court has jurisdiction over a particular criminal case depends on the intersection of several factors:

  • Subject matter: Does the offence fall within the exclusive catalogue of the Audiencia Nacional (terrorism, cross-provincial economic crime, etc.)? If so, the case goes there regardless of where the conduct occurred.
  • Severity: If the Audiencia Nacional does not have jurisdiction, the sentencing threshold determines whether the case is tried by a Juzgado de lo Penal (up to five years) or the Audiencia Provincial (over five years).
  • Territory: Among courts at the same level, jurisdiction is determined by the place where the offence was committed — the forum delicti commissi. For offences committed in multiple locations, the court where the offence was first detected or where the primary criminal act occurred typically takes jurisdiction.
  • Personal status: Aforamiento rules direct cases involving certain officials to higher courts, as described above.

Jurisdictional disputes — where two or more courts claim or disclaim jurisdiction over the same case — are resolved by the immediately superior common court, or by the Sala de Conflictos de Jurisdicción (Jurisdictional Conflicts Chamber) in complex situations. These disputes are more common than one might expect, particularly in economic crime cases where the conduct spans multiple provinces.

What Does This Mean for International Firms?

Several practical implications flow from Spain's court structure:

  • Identify the court early: The first question in any Spanish criminal engagement should be: which court has jurisdiction? This determines the procedural rules, the judges involved, the geographic location of hearings, and the appellate pathway.
  • Understand the investigative separation: The investigating judge and the trial judge are never the same person. This means the investigative phase is your primary opportunity to shape the case file — what goes in, and what is excluded.
  • Anticipate the appellate route: Appeals from Juzgados de lo Penal go to the Audiencia Provincial; appeals from Audiencias Provinciales and the Audiencia Nacional go to the Tribunal Supremo via casación; constitutional complaints go to the Tribunal Constitucional. Planning the appellate strategy begins at trial.
  • Factor in geographic logistics: Spain is a large country, and proceedings take place in the court with territorial jurisdiction. A case investigated in Malaga will be tried in Malaga — not in Madrid, unless the Audiencia Nacional has jurisdiction. For international firms, this may require coordination with local counsel in the relevant city.
  • Know the Audiencia Nacional: If your client is involved in a matter with cross-border dimensions — international fraud, money laundering, drug trafficking — the Audiencia Nacional is likely to be the relevant court. Its procedures and culture differ somewhat from provincial courts, and familiarity with its specific practices is advantageous.
