What Is a Conformidad? Plea Agreements in Spanish Criminal Law

By Muralla AI | Last updated: May 2026 | 12 min read
TL;DR A conformidad (plea agreement) is Spain's primary mechanism for resolving criminal cases without a full juicio oral. The defendant agrees to the facts, the legal classification of the offence, and the proposed sentence. Spanish law provides two main forms: ordinary conformidad under Art. 787 of the Ley de Enjuiciamiento Criminal (LECrim) (Criminal Procedure Act), and fast-track conformidad under Art. 801 LECrim, which offers a one-third sentence reduction. Unlike common law plea bargains, conformidad in Spain is subject to strict judicial oversight and sentencing limits.

International law firms advising clients in Spanish criminal proceedings will inevitably encounter the conformidad (plea agreement). Roughly comparable to plea bargaining in common law jurisdictions, the conformidad allows the defendant to accept a negotiated outcome and avoid the uncertainty, cost, and duration of a full oral hearing. In practice, conformidades resolve a substantial proportion of Spanish criminal cases, particularly in the procedimiento abreviado (abbreviated procedure) that governs the majority of mid-range offences.

Yet the conformidad is not simply a Spanish-language plea bargain. It operates within a fundamentally different procedural framework, with distinct rules about when it can be offered, what the court must verify, and how the resulting sentence is determined. This article explains those rules and their strategic implications for cross-border practitioners.

What Exactly Is a Conformidad?

At its core, a conformidad is the defendant's acceptance of three elements: the facts as described by the prosecution, the legal qualification of those facts as a specific criminal offence, and the sentence proposed. When the defendant — through their lawyer, and with their personal and express consent — agrees to all three elements, the court may issue a sentencia de conformidad (conformity judgment) without proceeding to the full evidential phase of the juicio oral.

The conformidad is not a contract between prosecution and defence in the way that plea agreements function in the United States. Rather, it is a procedural act that requires judicial validation. The court retains the power to reject a conformidad if it considers the facts do not constitute a criminal offence, if the sentence proposed is disproportionate, or if there are indications that the defendant's consent is not genuine.

What Are the Two Main Types of Conformidad?

Ordinary Conformidad: Art. 787 LECrim

Article 787 of the LECrim governs the standard conformidad available in proceedings conducted through the procedimiento abreviado (abbreviated procedure). This is the workhorse of Spanish plea practice. The key requirements are:

  • The sentence agreed upon must not exceed six years' imprisonment. This ceiling applies to the most serious individual offence charged, not the aggregate of all charges.
  • The defendant must give their personal and express consent after being informed of the consequences. It is not sufficient for the lawyer alone to agree — the court must verify that the defendant understands and accepts.
  • The conformidad may be reached at any point from the filing of the escrito de acusación (indictment) through to the beginning of the juicio oral. In practice, many conformidades are negotiated in the corridors of the courthouse on the day of the hearing itself.
  • The court must verify that the facts described constitute a criminal offence and that the sentence is lawful. If the court considers the sentence to be manifestly inadequate in light of the facts, it may reject the conformidad — though in practice, courts rarely exercise this power.

Fast-Track Conformidad: Art. 801 LECrim

Article 801 applies specifically to cases processed through the juicio rápido (fast-track trial) procedure, introduced by reforms in 2002 to expedite straightforward criminal matters. The juicio rápido covers offences such as minor assaults, drink driving, petty theft, and similar cases where the investigation can be completed within the initial police detention period.

The distinctive feature of Art. 801 is its incentive structure: if the defendant agrees to a conformidad before the case is sent for oral hearing, the sentence is automatically reduced by one third. This reduction is mandatory — it is not a matter of judicial discretion. Additionally, if the reduced sentence is two years' imprisonment or less, and the defendant has no prior criminal record, the court will typically suspend the sentence entirely, meaning no actual imprisonment results.

The combination of mandatory reduction and likely suspension makes Art. 801 conformidad an extremely attractive option in appropriate cases. For international clients facing charges in fast-track proceedings — particularly in matters arising from incidents during travel, such as altercations, minor property offences, or traffic matters — this mechanism often represents the most pragmatic path forward.

How Does Conformidad Differ From Common Law Plea Bargaining?

The structural differences are significant, and international practitioners should be aware of each:

  • No charge bargaining: In common law systems, plea negotiations frequently involve the prosecution dropping or reducing charges in exchange for a guilty plea. Spanish conformidad, by contrast, operates primarily on sentence rather than charge. The Fiscalía may adjust its sentencing request within the legal range, but wholesale charge reduction is uncommon and procedurally constrained.
  • Judicial control: American plea bargains are subject to judicial approval, but the judge's role is generally limited. In Spain, the court has an affirmative duty to verify that the conformidad is legally sound and that the defendant's consent is informed and voluntary. The court may — and occasionally does — reject agreed sentences that it considers unlawful.
  • No allocution requirement: Common law guilty pleas typically require the defendant to describe the offence in their own words. Spanish conformidad requires only that the defendant accept the facts as described by the prosecution, without narrating them independently.
  • Limited withdrawal rights: Once a sentencia de conformidad is issued, avenues for reversal are extremely narrow. The defendant cannot simply change their mind, as might be possible in some common law jurisdictions before sentencing. Appeals are limited to arguments that consent was vitiated or that the sentence exceeded legal limits.
  • Role of the victim: If an acusación particular (private prosecution) is involved, negotiations become tripartite. The victim's lawyer may agree to or oppose the proposed conformidad, and their position on civil liability — the responsabilidad civil (civil liability) component of the criminal case — is an integral part of any negotiated resolution.

What Role Does the Fiscalía Play in Conformidad Negotiations?

The Fiscalía (Public Prosecution Service) is the primary counterparty in conformidad negotiations, but its role differs meaningfully from that of a common law prosecutor. Spanish prosecutors are bound by the principle of legalidad (legality) — they must apply the law as written and cannot offer concessions that fall outside the legally permissible sentencing range for the offence charged.

In practice, this means conformidad negotiations tend to focus on three levers: the specific subtipo (sub-category) of the offence that applies to the facts, the presence or absence of circunstancias atenuantes (mitigating circumstances) such as confession or reparation of harm, and the precise point within the sentencing range that the Fiscalía will request. The application of a mitigating circumstance can lower the applicable sentencing range significantly, creating space for a conformidad at a sentence the defendant finds acceptable.

Importantly, individual prosecutors in Spain have less discretionary authority than their counterparts in many common law systems. Conformidad proposals in complex or high-profile cases often require approval from senior prosecutors within the Fiscalía's internal hierarchy. International firms should factor this institutional structure into their negotiation timelines.

When Should a Defendant Accept a Conformidad?

The decision to accept a conformidad involves weighing several factors:

  • Strength of the evidence: If the investigative file contains strong evidence of guilt, the marginal benefit of proceeding to trial may be limited. Conformidad allows the defendant to secure a known outcome rather than risk a harsher sentence after a contested hearing.
  • Sentence suspension: Under Art. 80 of the Código Penal (Criminal Code), prison sentences of two years or less may be suspended for first-time offenders. If a conformidad produces a sentence within this threshold, the practical result may be no imprisonment at all — a powerful incentive.
  • Civil liability: Conformidad often includes an agreement on responsabilidad civil (civil damages). Resolving both criminal and civil liability in a single proceeding can be significantly more efficient than contesting the criminal case and then facing a separate civil claim.
  • Reputational considerations: For international clients, a swift resolution through conformidad may be preferable to protracted proceedings that generate ongoing publicity. The conformidad hearing itself is typically brief and attracts little public attention.
  • Immigration consequences: Non-EU nationals should be advised that a criminal conviction — even through conformidad — may have immigration implications under Spanish law, including potential expulsión (deportation) as a substitute for imprisonment in certain cases.

What Are the Risks and Limitations of Conformidad?

The principal risk is finality. A sentencia de conformidad is a criminal conviction in every sense. It creates a criminal record, it may trigger collateral consequences — professional disqualification, loss of firearms licences, immigration repercussions — and it is very difficult to undo. Defence lawyers must ensure their clients understand that conformidad is not an administrative resolution or a slap on the wrist: it is a guilty verdict with a sentence attached.

Another limitation is the six-year ceiling under Art. 787. For offences carrying potential sentences above six years, the ordinary conformidad mechanism is unavailable. In such cases, the defendant must proceed to a full juicio oral, though informal sentencing indications from the court — while not formally part of Spanish procedure — sometimes occur in practice.

Finally, multi-defendant cases present particular complications. Each defendant must individually consent, and the interests of co-defendants may diverge. A conformidad by one defendant does not bind the others, and the testimony of a conforming defendant may subsequently be used as evidence against those who proceed to trial.
