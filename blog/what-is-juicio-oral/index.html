What Is a Juicio Oral? The Spanish Criminal Hearing Explained

By Muralla AI | Last updated: May 2026 | 12 min read
TL;DR A juicio oral (oral hearing) is the central stage of a Spanish criminal trial. It is the formal courtroom proceeding where all evidence is presented orally, witnesses testify under examination, and the court hears final arguments before issuing a verdict. For international lawyers, the juicio oral represents the most critical procedural divergence from common law trial practice: there is no jury in most cases, the judge plays an active investigative role, and the entire proceeding is shaped by a prior written investigation phase that has no direct equivalent in adversarial systems.

If your firm is handling a Spanish criminal matter for the first time, the juicio oral (oral hearing) is the proceeding you need to understand most thoroughly. It is not simply a Spanish-language version of an Anglo-American trial. The juicio oral operates under fundamentally different procedural logic, shaped by Spain's inquisitorial tradition and codified primarily in the Ley de Enjuiciamiento Criminal (LECrim) (Criminal Procedure Act), the statute that has governed Spanish criminal procedure since 1882.

This guide walks through the structure, participants, phases, and practical implications of the juicio oral for international practitioners advising clients in Spain.

How Does the Juicio Oral Fit Into the Spanish Criminal Process?

Spanish criminal proceedings are divided into two broad stages. The first is the fase de instrucción (investigative phase), conducted by the Juez de Instrucción (investigating judge). During this phase, the judge gathers evidence, hears preliminary testimony, and determines whether there is sufficient basis to proceed to trial. This is an entirely judicial function — not a prosecutorial one, as common law lawyers might expect.

Once the investigating judge issues an auto de apertura de juicio oral (order opening the oral hearing), the case moves to a different court for the juicio oral itself. The separation is structural: the judge who investigated the case is never the judge who presides over the hearing. This principle, rooted in constitutional jurisprudence, protects the defendant's right to an impartial tribunal.

For lawyers accustomed to adversarial systems, this bifurcation means that the juicio oral begins with an extensive written record already in place. The presiding judge has access to the full investigative file, or sumario (case file). Understanding what is in that file — and what is not — is essential preparation.

Who Participates in a Juicio Oral?

The participants in a juicio oral reflect Spain's unique procedural structure:

  • The presiding judge or tribunal: Depending on the severity of the offence, the hearing may be conducted by a single judge at a Juzgado de lo Penal (Criminal Court) for offences carrying up to five years' imprisonment, or by a three-judge panel at the Audiencia Provincial (Provincial Court) for more serious matters.
  • The Ministerio Fiscal (Public Prosecution Service): Often referred to as the Fiscalía, the prosecution represents the public interest. Unlike in common law systems, the Fiscalía has an obligation of objectivity — it must request acquittal if it believes the evidence does not support conviction.
  • The acusación particular (private prosecution): Spanish law permits the victim to participate as a party to the proceedings through their own lawyer. This is a full prosecutorial role, not merely a victim impact statement.
  • The acusación popular (popular prosecution): Any Spanish citizen may bring a criminal prosecution in cases involving public interest, even without being a direct victim. This distinctive feature of Spanish law occasionally produces complex multi-party hearings.
  • The defence: The abogado defensor (defence lawyer) represents the accused. The defendant has a constitutional right to effective legal representation throughout.
  • The defendant: Known as the acusado (accused), the defendant must be present throughout the juicio oral, except in proceedings for minor offences where the court may authorise absence.

What Are the Phases of a Juicio Oral?

Phase 1: Cuestiones Previas (Preliminary Issues)

Before any evidence is taken, the court addresses cuestiones previas (preliminary issues). These may include challenges to the court's jurisdiction, objections to the admissibility of evidence, requests to exclude particular witnesses, or arguments regarding procedural defects in the investigative phase. This phase is procedurally critical — issues not raised here may be deemed waived for the purposes of any subsequent appeal.

International lawyers should pay particular attention to this phase. If evidence was obtained in another jurisdiction through mutual legal assistance, its admissibility must often be contested at this stage. Similarly, if the investigative phase involved questionable procedures — such as irregular wiretaps or problematic search warrants — the cuestiones previas is the moment to challenge them.

Phase 2: Práctica de Prueba (Taking of Evidence)

The práctica de prueba (taking of evidence) is the longest and most substantive phase. It typically proceeds in the following order:

  1. Interrogation of the defendant: The accused is questioned first, beginning with the prosecution and followed by the defence. The defendant has the right to remain silent — the derecho a no declarar contra sí mismo (right not to testify against oneself) — and silence cannot be used as evidence of guilt.
  2. Witness testimony: Witnesses are examined by the party that proposed them, then cross-examined by the opposing parties. The presiding judge may also pose questions directly, a feature that often surprises common law practitioners.
  3. Expert evidence: Expert witnesses, or peritos (court experts), present their findings and are subject to questioning by all parties.
  4. Documentary and material evidence: Relevant documents, recordings, and physical evidence are formally introduced and examined.

A crucial difference from common law practice: Spanish courts apply the principle of libre valoración de la prueba (free evaluation of evidence). There are no formal rules of evidence comparable to the Federal Rules of Evidence or the hearsay rule. The judge evaluates all evidence according to reason and experience, though constitutional case law requires that convictions be based on evidence that is both lawfully obtained and sufficient to overcome the presumption of innocence.

Phase 3: Informes (Oral Arguments)

After the evidence has been taken, the parties present their informes (oral arguments). The prosecution speaks first, followed by any private prosecution, and finally the defence. Each party summarises its case, analyses the evidence presented during the hearing, and requests a specific verdict and sentence.

At this stage, parties may modify their initial qualification of the facts — a process known as modificación de conclusiones (modification of conclusions). This means the prosecution or defence may adjust the specific criminal classification of the conduct, the aggravating or mitigating circumstances alleged, or the sentence requested, based on what emerged during the práctica de prueba.

Phase 4: Última Palabra (The Last Word)

The juicio oral concludes with the derecho a la última palabra (right to the last word). The presiding judge asks the defendant directly whether they wish to say anything. This is not a procedural formality — failure to offer the defendant this right constitutes a ground for annulment of the entire proceeding on appeal. The defendant may make any statement they wish without being subject to cross-examination.

How Does the Juicio Oral Differ From a Common Law Trial?

Several structural differences deserve emphasis for international practitioners:

  • No jury in most cases: The Tribunal del Jurado (jury court) exists in Spain but only for a limited catalogue of offences, including homicide, threats, and certain offences by public officials. The vast majority of criminal cases are decided by professional judges.
  • Active judicial role: The presiding judge is not a passive referee. They may question witnesses, request clarification, and direct the taking of evidence. This reflects the inquisitorial heritage of the system.
  • No formal opening statements: The juicio oral begins directly with the cuestiones previas and then proceeds to evidence. There is no equivalent of the common law opening statement.
  • Written investigative record: The judge has already reviewed the investigative file before the hearing begins. While the oral hearing is where evidence must be formally presented for it to have probative value, the prior written record shapes the entire proceeding.
  • Principle of orality and immediacy: Despite the written background, the juicio oral is governed by the principles of oralidad (orality) and inmediación (immediacy) — meaning that the court must hear evidence directly and in person for it to serve as the basis of a conviction.

How Should International Firms Prepare for a Juicio Oral?

Effective preparation requires understanding that the juicio oral is not the beginning of the case — it is the culmination of months or years of investigation. By the time the hearing date arrives, the investigative file may contain thousands of pages of documentation, witness statements, expert reports, and judicial orders.

International firms should focus on several priorities:

  • Master the investigative file: Every document in the sumario is potential material for the hearing. Identifying inconsistencies between pre-trial statements and live testimony is a fundamental defence technique.
  • Prepare for preliminary challenges: Evidence admissibility issues must be identified and briefed before the hearing. The cuestiones previas phase moves quickly and leaves little room for improvisation.
  • Coordinate with Spanish counsel: Only lawyers registered with a Spanish Colegio de Abogados (Bar Association) may appear in Spanish courts. International firms must work through qualified local counsel.
  • Anticipate the modification of conclusions: The prosecution's case may shift during the hearing. Prepare for multiple scenarios, not just the charges as initially framed.
