Prescription: Limitation Periods in Spanish Criminal Law

By Muralla AI | Last updated: May 2026 | 15 min read
TL;DR Prescripción (criminal limitation / prescription) is the legal principle that bars prosecution of an offence after a specified period has elapsed. Under Articles 131 and 132 of the Código Penal (Criminal Code), Spanish limitation periods range from one year for delitos leves (minor offences) to twenty years for offences carrying sentences of fifteen or more years' imprisonment. Genocide and crimes against humanity never prescribe. The clock starts on the date of commission and is interrupted when proceedings are judicially directed against the suspect. For international lawyers, mastering prescripción is essential — a viable limitation defence can end a case entirely.

Limitation periods exist in virtually every legal system, but their operation in Spanish criminal law has features that international practitioners must understand to advise clients competently. The Spanish concept of prescripción del delito (prescription of the offence) determines whether the state retains the power to prosecute at all. It is a substantive matter — meaning it can be raised at any stage of proceedings, including for the first time on appeal — and its successful invocation results in the automatic dismissal of the case.

This article provides a comprehensive overview of how prescription works under the current Código Penal (CP) (Criminal Code), with particular attention to the issues most relevant to cross-border criminal matters.

What Are the Limitation Periods Under Spanish Law?

Article 131 of the Código Penal establishes the following limitation periods, determined by the maximum penalty prescribed for the offence in question:

Maximum SentenceLimitation Period
15 years or more imprisonment20 years
More than 10 and less than 15 years15 years
More than 5 and up to 10 years10 years
More than 3 and up to 5 years5 years
3 years or less / other penalties5 years
Delitos leves (minor offences)1 year

Critically, the relevant period is determined by the abstract maximum sentence prescribed by law for the offence type, not by the specific sentence the court would likely impose in the particular case. This distinction matters enormously in practice. An offence that might realistically result in a two-year sentence could carry a five-year statutory maximum, placing it in a longer limitation bracket.

Furthermore, Article 131.4 provides that certain offences are imprescriptible — they have no limitation period whatsoever. This applies to genocide, crimes against humanity, and offences against persons and property protected in the event of armed conflict, reflecting Spain's obligations under international law.

When Does the Limitation Clock Start Running?

The dies a quo (starting date) of the limitation period is governed by Art. 132.1 CP, which establishes the general rule: the clock begins on the day the criminal conduct was committed. However, the application of this apparently simple principle varies depending on the nature of the offence:

Instantaneous Offences

For delitos instantáneos (instantaneous offences) — those completed by a single act at a specific moment — the limitation period begins on the date of that act. A theft committed on 15 March 2020, for example, begins prescribing on that date.

Continuous and Continued Offences

For delitos continuados (continued offences) — a series of related criminal acts treated as a single offence under Art. 74 CP — the period begins from the date of the last criminal act in the series. This is particularly relevant in fraud, embezzlement, and other economic crimes where the criminal conduct extends over months or years.

Similarly, for delitos permanentes (permanent offences) — where the criminal situation persists over time, such as unlawful detention — the clock does not start until the criminal situation ceases. A kidnapping victim held for six months triggers the limitation period only from the date of their release.

Result Offences

For offences that require a specific result — such as homicide or certain forms of injury — the prevailing interpretation is that the limitation period begins when the result occurs, not when the causative act was performed. If a poisoning is administered in January but the victim dies in March, the clock starts in March.

The Problem of Discovery in Economic Crimes

One of the most contested areas of Spanish criminal prescription concerns offences that are inherently difficult to detect. Tax fraud, corporate mismanagement, money laundering, and other economic crimes may be committed years before anyone becomes aware of them. Spanish law does not contain a general discovery rule equivalent to what exists in some civil law contexts or in certain common law statutes of limitation.

However, judicial practice has developed interpretive approaches that effectively delay the dies a quo in certain economic crime scenarios. The Tribunal Supremo (Supreme Court) has held that in cases of concealment, the limitation period may begin from the moment the offence becomes objectively discoverable, rather than the date of commission. The precise contours of this doctrine remain subject to ongoing jurisprudential development and are frequently litigated.

For international firms handling cross-border economic crime matters, this area demands careful analysis. The interaction between the formal statutory rules and the evolving case law on discovery can be outcome-determinative.

What Interrupts the Limitation Period?

Article 132.2 CP provides the core interruption rule: the limitation period is interrupted when el procedimiento se dirija contra la persona indiciariamente responsable del delito — that is, when judicial proceedings are directed against the person suspected of responsibility for the offence.

This formulation raises several interpretive questions that have generated extensive case law:

What Constitutes "Directing" Proceedings Against the Suspect?

The Tribunal Supremo has established that the interrupting act must be a judicial act — not a prosecutorial or police act. The filing of a criminal complaint (denuncia) or a formal accusation (querella) by the victim or the Ministerio Fiscal (prosecution) does not, by itself, interrupt the limitation period. What interrupts it is the subsequent judicial resolution that identifies the individual as an investigado (person under investigation) and opens or continues formal proceedings against them.

This distinction is critically important. In complex cases, months or even years may pass between the initial complaint and the judicial act that formally directs proceedings against a specific individual. During that entire period, the limitation clock continues to run.

Does Interruption Reset the Clock Entirely?

Yes. Under the current interpretation, once the limitation period is interrupted by a judicial act directed against the suspect, the entire period begins running anew from the date of interruption. This is sometimes referred to as interrupción con efecto reiniciador (interruption with reset effect). It means that in long-running proceedings, the limitation period is effectively restarted each time a significant judicial act occurs.

Can the Clock Resume After Interruption?

If proceedings are initiated but then become dormant — if no significant procedural steps are taken for an extended period — the limitation clock begins running again. This situation, known colloquially as paralización del procedimiento (procedural paralysis), can arise when cases languish in overburdened courts. If the period of inactivity exceeds the applicable limitation period, the offence prescribes despite the existence of formal proceedings.

This rule has practical significance for international cases, which often involve complex mutual legal assistance requests, translations, and coordination between jurisdictions — all of which can slow proceedings considerably.

How Does Prescription Apply to Penalties?

Distinct from the prescription of the offence itself, Spanish law also recognises prescripción de la pena (prescription of the penalty) under Art. 133-134 CP. This applies when a sentence has been imposed but not executed — for instance, when a convicted person has fled before serving their sentence. The periods mirror those for offences but are calculated from the date of the final judgment.

For international practitioners, penalty prescription becomes relevant when a client is discovered abroad years after a Spanish conviction. If the penalty has prescribed, enforcement is no longer possible, regardless of the validity of the underlying judgment.

What Are the Strategic Implications for International Firms?

Prescription should be one of the first issues analysed in any Spanish criminal matter. Several strategic considerations deserve attention:

Early Assessment Is Critical

Because prescription is a substantive bar to prosecution — not merely a procedural objection — it can resolve a case entirely and at any stage. International firms should calculate limitation dates as soon as they receive the case file. Identifying a viable prescription defence early can save the client significant cost and exposure.

The Classification of the Offence Matters

Since the limitation period depends on the maximum sentence for the offence, the legal classification of the conduct is directly relevant. A reclassification from a more serious to a less serious offence — or vice versa — changes the applicable limitation period. Defence lawyers should scrutinise the prosecution's legal qualification and consider whether a lower classification is arguable, particularly if it would bring the offence within a shorter limitation bracket.

Cross-Border Complications

Cases involving conduct that spans multiple jurisdictions raise difficult questions about when and where the limitation period begins, whether foreign proceedings interrupt the Spanish clock, and how mutual legal assistance timelines interact with prescription rules. The general principle is that only Spanish judicial proceedings interrupt Spanish limitation periods, but international cooperation mechanisms add layers of complexity that require specialist analysis.

Economic Crime Cases Require Special Attention

Given the ongoing judicial evolution around discovery dates in economic crime, firms handling tax fraud, money laundering, corporate crime, or insolvency offences should analyse prescription with particular care. The difference between the formal commission date and the judicially determined dies a quo can extend or collapse the limitation window by years.

Monitor for Procedural Paralysis

In long-running proceedings, defence lawyers should track periods of judicial inactivity. If the court fails to take meaningful procedural steps for a period approaching the limitation term, a prescription argument may become available — even in a case that was initially timely. This requires careful monitoring of the procedural record, a task well suited to automated tracking tools.
